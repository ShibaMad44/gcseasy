Atomic structure & the periodic table

  • Atomic number (bottom) = protons. In a neutral atom, protons = electrons.
  • Mass number (top) = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = mass − atomic. ²³₁₁Na → 11 p, 11 e, 12 n.
  • Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons.
  • Ions: same protons, different electrons. Lose e⁻ → positive; gain e⁻ → negative. Mg²⁺ has 10 electrons.
  • Atoms have no overall charge because protons = electrons.

Relative atomic mass (Ar)

Ar = Σ(abundance × mass number) ÷ total abundance

Chlorine: 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl → (75×35 + 25×37) ÷ 100 = 35.5

History of the atom

  • Dalton: atoms are tiny solid spheres that can't be divided.
  • Thomson discovered the electron → plum pudding model (ball of positive charge with electrons embedded).
  • Rutherford, alpha scattering: most alpha particles went straight through → mostly empty space. A few deflected → positive charge concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Nuclear model replaced plum pudding.
  • Bohr: electrons orbit the nucleus at fixed distances (shells).
  • Protons found in the nucleus; Chadwick proved neutrons exist.

Discovery order: electron → proton → neutron.

Groups

  • Group 1 alkali metals: 1 outer electron. React with water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. More reactive going down: outer electron further from nucleus, weaker attraction, lost more easily.
  • Group 7 halogens: 7 outer electrons, exist as pairs (Cl₂). Less reactive going down: harder to gain an electron. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one: chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine.
  • Group 0 noble gases: full outer shell, so unreactive. Boiling points increase going down.
Watch out: some books call it "Group 8". Write Group 0 in the exam.

In depth: 6-mark practice

"Describe how the model of the atom has changed over time."
  1. Dalton (early 1800s): atoms are tiny solid spheres that can't be divided.
  2. Thomson (1897) discovered the electron, giving the plum pudding model: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
  3. Rutherford's alpha scattering experiment: alpha particles were fired at thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, so the atom is mostly empty space. A few were deflected or bounced back, so there's a tiny, dense, positive nucleus. This gave the nuclear model.
  4. Bohr showed electrons orbit the nucleus at fixed distances in shells (energy levels).
  5. Later the nucleus was shown to contain protons, and Chadwick (1932) discovered the neutron.
Each model changed because new evidence from experiments didn't fit the old one.
"Explain the trend in reactivity in Group 1." All Group 1 metals have 1 electron in their outer shell, which they lose to form 1+ ions. Going down the group, atoms get bigger with more shells, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded. The attraction is weaker, so the electron is lost more easily and reactivity increases. Observations with water: lithium fizzes, sodium melts into a ball and fizzes, potassium burns with a lilac flame. All make an alkaline hydroxide and hydrogen.
"Explain the trend in reactivity in Group 7." Halogens have 7 outer electrons and gain 1 to form 1− ions. Down the group, atoms get bigger, so the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded. An electron is gained less easily, so reactivity decreases. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from a salt solution, e.g. chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine (the solution turns orange).
Required practical: "Describe how to use paper chromatography to identify the inks in a sample."
  1. Draw a pencil start line 1–2 cm from the bottom of the paper (pencil won't dissolve).
  2. Put small spots of the unknown ink and known inks on the line.
  3. Stand the paper in a beaker of solvent (water), with the solvent level below the line so the spots don't dissolve into it. Put a lid on.
  4. Remove it when the solvent is near the top and mark the solvent front. Leave to dry.
  5. Calculate Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent.
  6. Compare with the known inks: matching Rf values and colours identify them. One spot means a pure substance.
"Describe how to separate a mixture" (e.g. salt from rock salt, or water from ink). Rock salt: dissolve in warm water, filter to remove the sand, then crystallise by evaporating some water and leaving it to cool. Ink: simple distillation (heat, vapour condenses in the cooled condenser, pure water collected). Mixture of liquids with different boiling points: fractional distillation.

Bonding & structure

Finding bonding hard? Open the Bonding deep dive at the end of this section. It teaches C2 step by step with diagrams.
BondBetweenWhat happens
IonicMetal + non-metalElectrons transferred → oppositely charged ions attract
CovalentNon-metal + non-metalPairs of electrons shared
MetallicMetal atomsPositive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons; strong attraction between them

Making NaCl: sodium loses 1 electron → Na⁺; chlorine gains 1 electron → Cl⁻. Both now have a full outer shell. The ions are held together by strong electrostatic attraction.

Giant ionic lattice (e.g. NaCl)

  • Strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions, in all directions.
  • High melting/boiling points: lots of energy needed to overcome them.
  • Conduct only when molten or dissolved: ions are free to move and carry charge.

Simple molecular (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, O₂)

  • Low melting/boiling points: only weak intermolecular forces are overcome, not the covalent bonds.
  • Don't conduct: no free electrons or ions, no overall charge.

Giant covalent (diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide)

  • Very high melting points: many strong covalent bonds must break.
  • Diamond: each C has 4 covalent bonds → very hard, doesn't conduct.
  • Graphite: each C has 3 bonds, in layers. Weak forces between layers → slide, soft. One delocalised electron per C → conducts.

Polymers

Many small monomers join into long-chain polymers.

Metals & alloys

  • High melting points, good conductors (delocalised electrons).
  • Malleable: layers of atoms slide over each other.
  • Alloys are harder: different-sized atoms distort the layers, so they can't slide as easily.

How the bonds form: dot-and-cross

Ionic: electrons are transferred

  • Sodium (2,8,1) loses its 1 outer electron → Na⁺ (2,8).
  • Chlorine (2,8,7) gains 1 electron → Cl⁻ (2,8,8). The cross is the electron from sodium.
  • Both now have a full outer shell. Draw ions in square brackets with the charge outside.
  • Magnesium oxide: Mg (2,8,2) loses 2 electrons → Mg²⁺; O (2,6) gains 2 → O²⁻.

Working out formulas: make the charges cancel. Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ need two Cl⁻ → MgCl₂. Al³⁺ and O²⁻: 2 × 3+ = 6+ and 3 × 2− = 6− → Al₂O₃.

Covalent: electrons are shared

  • Each covalent bond = one shared pair of electrons, drawn where the circles overlap.
  • Water: O shares one pair with each H → 2 bonds. Methane CH₄ → 4 bonds. Cl₂ → 1 bond.
  • Only draw the outer shells.

Limits of these diagrams (a common 1-mark question): dot-and-cross diagrams don't show the 3D arrangement or the relative sizes of atoms.

"Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point and conducts only when molten or dissolved." NaCl is a giant ionic lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Strong electrostatic forces act in all directions between oppositely charged ions, so lots of energy is needed to break them, hence a high melting point. As a solid the ions are fixed in place, so it can't conduct. When molten or dissolved the ions are free to move and carry charge.
"Explain why simple molecular substances have low melting points and don't conduct." The covalent bonds inside molecules are strong, but the intermolecular forces between molecules are weak, so little energy is needed to separate them. Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so higher boiling points. There are no ions or free electrons, so they don't conduct.
"Explain why metals conduct and can be bent, and why alloys are harder." Metals are a giant lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. The electrons move and carry charge and heat. The layers of ions can slide over each other, so metals are malleable. In an alloy, different-sized atoms distort the layers, so they can't slide easily, making it harder.

C3Mo

Quantitative chemistry

  • Ar = relative atomic mass (big number on the periodic table).
  • Mr = sum of the Ar of every atom in the formula.
MgCl₂ = 24 + (2 × 35.5) = 95    Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2×(16+1) = 74

Moles

moles = mass ÷ Mr  |  mass = moles × Mr

1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's constant).

Mass from an equation: 4 steps

  1. Moles of what you know = mass ÷ Mr
  2. Use the big numbers for the ratio
  3. Moles of what you want
  4. Mass = moles × Mr
2NaClO₃ → 2NaCl + 3O₂ : 0.4 mol NaClO₃ → 0.6 mol O₂ → 0.6 × 32 = 19.2 g

Limiting reactant: used up first, so it limits how much product forms. The other is in excess.

Conservation of mass

  • Mass seems to increase if a reactant is a gas from the air (Mg + O₂ from air → MgO).
  • Mass seems to decrease if a product is a gas that escapes (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).

Concentration

concentration (g/dm³) = mass (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
Watch out: it's volume of solution, not solvent. cm³ → dm³: ÷ 1000.

In depth: 6-mark practice

"Explain why the mass seems to change in some reactions." Mass is always conserved: atoms aren't made or destroyed. If a gas is given off into the air (e.g. thermal decomposition of a carbonate: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂), the measured mass decreases. If a gas from the air reacts (e.g. metal + oxygen → metal oxide), the mass increases. In a sealed container, the mass stays the same.
"Calculate the mass of product" (4 steps). 1) Moles of the known substance = mass ÷ Mr. 2) Use the ratio from the balanced equation. 3) Mass of product = moles × Mr. 4) Give units and sensible significant figures. Show every step, because method marks are given.
C4pH

Chemical changes

0 strong acid7 neutral14 strong alkali
  • Universal indicator: red/orange acid, green neutral, blue/purple alkali.
  • Acids → H⁺ ions in water. Alkalis → OH⁻ ions in water.
  • Base: neutralises an acid → salt + water. Alkali: a base that dissolves in water.
  • Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O
  • Strong acid: completely ionises in water (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃). Weak: partially ionises (ethanoic acid).

Acid reactions and naming salts

  • acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
  • acid + metal oxide/hydroxide → salt + water
  • acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
  • Hydrochloric → chloride; sulfuric → sulfate; nitric → nitrate. Zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen.

Making a soluble salt (required practical)

Copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate + water. Warm acid → add oxide until excess → filter → heat filtrate to evaporate some water → leave to crystallise → dry.

Redox: OIL RIG

  • Oxidation Is Loss of electrons. Reduction Is Gain.
  • Mg + CuSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Cu: Mg loses electrons (oxidised), Cu²⁺ gains them (reduced).

Electrolysis

  • Electrolyte must be molten or dissolved so ions can move.
  • Positive ions → cathode (−), gain electrons → reduced.
  • Negative ions → anode (+), lose electrons → oxidised.
  • Molten lead bromide: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb at cathode; 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ at anode.

Trick: PANIC – Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.

Reactivity series and extracting metals

K · Na · Li · Ca · Mg · C · Zn · Fe · H · Cu · Ag · Au
  • More reactive metals lose electrons (form positive ions) more easily. They react faster with water and acid.
  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its compound: magnesium + copper sulfate → magnesium sulfate + copper.
  • Metals below carbon (zinc, iron, copper) are extracted by heating the oxide with carbon. The oxide loses oxygen, so it's reduced.
  • Metals above carbon (e.g. aluminium) are extracted by electrolysis, which uses a lot of energy.
  • Gold is so unreactive it's found as the metal itself.
  • Oxidation can also mean gaining oxygen; reduction can mean losing oxygen.

Electrolysis of solutions

  • Water gives extra H⁺ and OH⁻ ions.
  • Cathode: the metal forms only if it's less reactive than hydrogen; otherwise hydrogen.
  • Anode: a halogen forms if there are halide ions; otherwise oxygen.
  • See the Practicals tab for the full method and gas tests.

In depth: 6-mark practice

Required practical: "Describe how to make pure, dry crystals of copper sulfate from copper oxide."
  1. Warm dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker (it reacts faster).
  2. Add copper oxide a spatula at a time, stirring, until it's in excess (no more dissolves, black solid stays). This uses up all the acid.
  3. Filter to remove the excess copper oxide.
  4. Heat the solution in an evaporating basin over a water bath to evaporate some water until crystals start to form.
  5. Leave it somewhere warm to crystallise slowly, then pat the blue crystals dry with filter paper.
Equation: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O.
Required practical: "Describe how to investigate the electrolysis of aqueous solutions." Pour the solution (e.g. copper chloride, sodium chloride) into a beaker. Put in two inert graphite electrodes that don't touch, and connect them to a d.c. supply (about 4 V). Observe each electrode. Test gases: chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper, hydrogen pops with a lighted splint, oxygen relights a glowing splint. A pink-brown coating on the cathode is copper.
"Explain what forms at each electrode in the electrolysis of a solution." Cathode (−): the metal forms if it's less reactive than hydrogen (e.g. copper). Otherwise hydrogen forms. Anode (+): if halide ions are present, the halogen forms (e.g. chlorine). Otherwise oxygen forms (from hydroxide ions). Positive ions gain electrons (reduction), negative ions lose electrons (oxidation): OIL RIG.
"Explain how aluminium is extracted, and why it's expensive." Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so it can't be extracted by reduction with carbon; electrolysis is needed. Aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite to lower the melting point, which saves energy. Al³⁺ goes to the cathode and gains electrons to form molten aluminium. O²⁻ goes to the graphite anode and forms oxygen, which reacts with the carbon to make CO₂, so the anodes wear away and must be replaced. It's expensive because of the large amounts of electricity and heat. Metals less reactive than carbon (e.g. iron) are extracted by heating with carbon, which is cheaper.
Triple: "Describe how to carry out a titration." Rinse and fill a burette with acid and read the start volume at eye level. Use a pipette to put 25.0 cm³ of alkali in a conical flask with a few drops of indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein). Add acid while swirling, then drop by drop near the end point until the colour just changes. Record the volume used. Repeat until you get concordant results (within 0.10 cm³) and take a mean.
C5En

Energy changes

  • Exothermic: energy released to surroundings, temperature rises. Burning, neutralisation, hand warmers.
  • Endothermic: energy taken in, temperature falls. Thermal decomposition, sports ice packs.
  • Breaking bonds takes in energy. Making bonds releases energy.
  • Activation energy: minimum energy needed for particles to react.
Energy change = bonds broken − bonds made  (negative → exothermic)

H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (H–H 436, Cl–Cl 243, H–Cl 432): broken = 679, made = 2 × 432 = 864 → 679 − 864 = −185 kJ/mol

Reaction profiles

  • Exothermic: products lower than reactants. Endothermic: products higher.
  • Activation energy: reactants up to the peak. Overall change: reactants to products.

Temperature change practical

Polystyrene cup with a lid (insulates, less heat loss), thermometer, measure start and highest/lowest temperature.

In depth: 6-mark practice

Required practical: "Describe how to investigate the temperature change when reacting an acid with an alkali."
  1. Put a polystyrene cup inside a beaker (for insulation) and add 30 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid.
  2. Record its starting temperature.
  3. Add 5 cm³ of sodium hydroxide, stir, put on a lid and record the highest temperature.
  4. Repeat, adding 5 cm³ at a time up to 40 cm³.
  5. Plot temperature against volume: the peak shows the volume needed to neutralise.
  6. Explain: the cup and lid reduce energy lost to the surroundings. Keep acid volume and concentration the same. Repeat for reliability.
"Explain the energy changes in exothermic and endothermic reactions using bonds." Energy is taken in to break bonds and given out when bonds form. Exothermic: more energy is released forming new bonds than is taken in breaking old ones, so the surroundings get warmer (combustion, neutralisation, hand warmers). Endothermic: more energy is needed to break bonds than is released, so the surroundings cool (thermal decomposition, sports injury packs). On a reaction profile, products are lower for exothermic and higher for endothermic. The hump is the activation energy.

Rates and equilibrium

Rate of reaction

  • Mean rate = quantity of reactant used ÷ time, or quantity of product formed ÷ time (units like g/s or cm³/s).
  • On a graph of product against time, the steeper the line, the faster the rate. HT Rate at a point = gradient of a tangent to the curve.
  • Collision theory: particles react only when they collide with at least the activation energy. Rate increases with more frequent collisions or more energetic ones.
FactorWhy the rate increases
Higher concentration (or pressure for gases)More particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions
Larger surface area (smaller pieces)More particles exposed, so more frequent collisions
Higher temperatureParticles move faster: more frequent collisions and more of them have the activation energy
CatalystProvides a different pathway with a lower activation energy; not used up

Reversible reactions and equilibrium

  • A reversible reaction can go both ways: A + B ⇌ C + D. Example: ammonium chloride ⇌ ammonia + hydrogen chloride.
  • If one direction is exothermic, the other is endothermic by the same amount. Example: hydrated copper sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper sulfate (white) + water; heating it is endothermic, adding water is exothermic.
  • In a closed system, equilibrium is reached when the forward and reverse reactions happen at exactly the same rate, so the amounts stay constant.
  • HT Le Chatelier's principle: if conditions change, the equilibrium shifts to counteract the change.
    • Increase a reactant's concentration → more product forms.
    • Increase temperature → equilibrium shifts in the endothermic direction.
    • Increase pressure (gases) → shifts to the side with fewer molecules.

In depth: 6-mark practice

Required practical: "Investigate how concentration affects the rate of reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid."
  1. Draw a black cross on paper and put a conical flask on top.
  2. Add a measured volume of sodium thiosulfate solution (e.g. 50 cm³).
  3. Add a set volume of hydrochloric acid (e.g. 5 cm³), swirl and start the stopwatch.
  4. Look down through the flask and stop the clock when the cross disappears (the solution goes cloudy as sulfur forms).
  5. Repeat with different concentrations of thiosulfate (dilute with water, keeping the total volume the same).
  6. Keep the temperature, acid volume and concentration, and the same cross and observer. Rate ∝ 1 ÷ time.
"Explain, using collision theory, why increasing temperature increases the rate." At a higher temperature particles have more kinetic energy and move faster. They collide more often, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so there are more successful collisions per second.
HT "Explain the effect of increasing pressure on an equilibrium." Count the gas molecules on each side. Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer molecules, so the yield of that side increases. If both sides have the same number, pressure has no effect.
C7Or

Organic chemistry

Crude oil and alkanes

  • Crude oil is a finite resource formed from the remains of ancient plankton buried in mud. It's a mixture of hydrocarbons (compounds of hydrogen and carbon only), mostly alkanes.
  • Alkanes: general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. The first four: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀. They're saturated (only single C–C bonds).
  • Fractional distillation: crude oil is heated until it evaporates. The vapour rises up a column that's hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top. Each fraction condenses at its own boiling point. Fractions: LPG, petrol, kerosene (jet fuel), diesel, heating oil, fuel oil, bitumen.
  • As chain length increases: boiling point rises, viscosity rises (thicker), flammability falls.
  • Complete combustion: hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. Releases energy.

Cracking and alkenes

  • Cracking breaks long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes. Catalytic cracking: vapour passed over a hot catalyst. Steam cracking: mixed with steam at a very high temperature.
  • Why: there's more demand for short-chain fuels (like petrol) than supply, and alkenes are used to make polymers.
  • Alkenes have a C=C double bond, so they're unsaturated. Test: bromine water turns from orange to colourless with an alkene (stays orange with an alkane).

Alcohols, acids and polymers Triple

  • Alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ) react with hydrogen, water, and halogens by adding across the double bond.
  • Alcohols (–OH): methanol, ethanol, propanol, butanol. Uses: fuels, solvents. Ethanol is made by fermentation of sugar with yeast.
  • Carboxylic acids (–COOH): e.g. ethanoic acid (vinegar). Weak acids. Alcohol + carboxylic acid → ester (sweet smell) + water.
  • Addition polymers form from many alkene monomers (e.g. ethene → poly(ethene)). Condensation polymers form from monomers with two functional groups, releasing a small molecule like water.
  • Natural polymers: DNA (nucleotides), proteins (amino acids), starch and cellulose (sugars).

In depth: 6-mark practice

"Describe how crude oil is separated by fractional distillation." Crude oil is heated until most of it evaporates. The vapour enters the bottom of a fractionating column, which is hot at the bottom and cool at the top. The vapour rises and cools. Each fraction condenses when it reaches the level where the temperature is just below its boiling point, and is collected. Long-chain hydrocarbons (high boiling points) condense near the bottom; short chains rise to the top. Bitumen stays as a liquid at the bottom.
"Explain why cracking is used." Fractional distillation produces more long-chain hydrocarbons than are needed and not enough short-chain ones like petrol, which are in high demand as fuels. Cracking breaks long chains into shorter, more useful alkanes (fuels) and alkenes, which are used to make polymers and other chemicals.

Chemical analysis

Purity and formulations

  • In chemistry, a pure substance is a single element or compound. Pure substances melt and boil at specific temperatures; impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a range.
  • A formulation is a mixture designed as a useful product, with each part in a measured amount: paints, medicines, fuels, cleaning agents, alloys, fertilisers, foods.
  • Chromatography separates mixtures. Pure substances give one spot. Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent.

Identifying ions Triple

  • Flame tests: lithium crimson, sodium yellow, potassium lilac, calcium orange-red, copper green.
  • Sodium hydroxide precipitates: Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ brown; Al³⁺, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ white (aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess).
  • Carbonates: add acid; CO₂ turns limewater milky. Halides: add nitric acid then silver nitrate: chloride white, bromide cream, iodide yellow. Sulfates: add hydrochloric acid then barium chloride: white precipitate.
  • Instrumental methods (e.g. flame emission spectroscopy) are accurate, sensitive and fast.

Chemistry of the atmosphere

How the atmosphere changed

  • Today: about 80% nitrogen, 20% oxygen, and small amounts of carbon dioxide (about 0.04%), water vapour and noble gases.
  • Early atmosphere (from volcanoes): mostly carbon dioxide, with water vapour, nitrogen and small amounts of methane and ammonia. Probably like Mars and Venus today.
  • Water vapour condensed to form the oceans. CO₂ dissolved in the oceans and formed carbonate precipitates (sediments).
  • Algae and plants made oxygen by photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂), starting about 2.7 billion years ago, and removed CO₂.
  • Carbon was locked up in sedimentary rocks (limestone) and fossil fuels (coal, crude oil, gas).

Greenhouse gases and climate change

  • Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour) let short-wavelength radiation from the Sun through but absorb the long-wavelength (infrared) radiation given off by the Earth, keeping it warm.
  • Human activity adds CO₂ (burning fossil fuels, deforestation) and methane (cattle, rice fields, landfill).
  • Effects of climate change: rising sea levels, more extreme weather, changes in rainfall, pressure on wildlife and food production.
  • Carbon footprint: the total greenhouse gases released over the life of a product, service or event. Reduced by renewable energy, energy efficiency, carbon capture, and less waste; hard because of cost, lifestyle and lack of international agreement.

In depth: 6-mark practice

"Describe how the Earth's atmosphere has changed since it formed." Early atmosphere came from volcanoes: mainly CO₂ with water vapour, nitrogen, and a little methane and ammonia. As the Earth cooled, water vapour condensed into oceans. CO₂ dissolved in the oceans, forming carbonates that became sedimentary rocks. Algae and plants evolved and used CO₂ for photosynthesis, releasing oxygen. Carbon was locked in fossil fuels and rocks. Now: about 80% nitrogen, 20% oxygen, a tiny amount of CO₂.
"Evaluate ways of reducing carbon emissions." Renewable energy (no CO₂ in use, but weather-dependent), energy-efficient homes, carbon capture and storage (expensive, still developing), planting trees, carbon taxes. Problems: cost, people's lifestyles, countries not agreeing. Judgement.

Using resources

Sustainability

  • Finite resources (crude oil, metal ores) will run out; renewable ones (timber, crops) can be replaced. Sustainable development meets today's needs without stopping future generations meeting theirs.

Water

  • Potable water is safe to drink (not pure: it contains dissolved substances). In the UK: choose a source of fresh water, filter it (wire mesh, then sand and gravel beds), then sterilise it (chlorine, ozone or UV light).
  • Desalination of sea water: distillation or reverse osmosis (membranes). Both need lots of energy.
  • Waste water treatment: screening and grit removal → sedimentation (sludge and effluent) → sludge broken down by anaerobic digestion → effluent treated with aerobic bacteria.
  • Required practical: test the pH of a water sample, then distil it and measure the mass of solids left after evaporating a sample.
  • HT Low-grade copper ores: phytomining (plants absorb copper, are burned, and copper is extracted from the ash) and bioleaching (bacteria produce solutions containing copper).

Life cycle assessment and recycling

  • A life cycle assessment (LCA) looks at the environmental impact at each stage: getting raw materials, manufacturing and packaging, use, and disposal. Some parts (e.g. pollution) are hard to give a value, so LCAs can be biased.
  • Reduce, reuse, recycle saves finite resources, energy and landfill space.

Materials and the Haber process Triple

  • Rusting needs water and oxygen. Prevent it with a barrier (paint, oil, plastic) or sacrificial protection (a more reactive metal like zinc: galvanising).
  • Alloys: bronze (copper + tin), brass (copper + zinc), steels, 18-carat gold (75% gold).
  • Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Conditions: about 450 °C, 200 atmospheres, iron catalyst. Unreacted gases are recycled. A compromise between yield and rate.
  • NPK fertilisers supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.

In depth: 6-mark practice

"Describe how water is made safe to drink in the UK." Choose a fresh water source (lake, river, ground water). Pass it through wire meshes to remove large objects, then through sand and gravel beds to filter out small solids. Sterilise it with chlorine, ozone or UV light to kill microbes. It's then potable: safe to drink, but not pure.
"Compare the life cycle assessments of plastic and paper bags." Go stage by stage: raw materials (crude oil vs trees), manufacturing (energy and pollution), use (plastic is reusable and stronger), disposal (paper biodegrades and recycles easily; plastic lasts in landfill). Give a judgement, and note that values like pollution are hard to measure.

Required practicals are worth around 15% of the marks, and 6-mark "describe a method" questions usually come from them. For each one, know the method, the variables and the safety points.

RP · Making a soluble salt

Example: copper sulfate from copper oxide and sulfuric acid. CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O

  1. Measure 40 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker and warm it gently with a Bunsen burner.
  2. Add copper oxide one spatula at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves. The oxide is now in excess, so all the acid has reacted.
  3. Filter to remove the excess copper oxide. Collect the blue filtrate in an evaporating basin.
  4. Heat the filtrate on a water bath until crystals start to form at the edge, then stop.
  5. Leave to cool and crystallise. Filter off the crystals and pat them dry with filter paper.
Why excess?
So all the acid is used up and the salt isn't contaminated with acid.
Why warm?
Speeds up the reaction.
Safety
Eye protection (acid irritant, copper sulfate harmful); don't heat to dryness (crystals can spit).
RP · Titration Triple
  1. Use a pipette and filler to measure exactly 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask.
  2. Add a few drops of indicator (phenolphthalein or methyl orange). Stand the flask on a white tile.
  3. Fill a burette with acid and record the starting volume at eye level.
  4. Add acid, swirling, then drop by drop near the end, until the indicator just changes colour (the end point).
  5. Record the final volume. Titre = final − start.
  6. Repeat until you get concordant results (within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and calculate a mean.
moles = concentration (mol/dm³) × volume (dm³)  HT
Indicator
Phenolphthalein: pink in alkali → colourless. Methyl orange: yellow in alkali → red in acid.
White tile
To see the colour change clearly.
Don't use universal indicator: it changes colour gradually, so there's no sharp end point.
RP · Electrolysis of aqueous solutions
  1. Pour the solution (e.g. copper chloride, sodium chloride) into a beaker.
  2. Put in two inert graphite electrodes that don't touch, connected to a low-voltage d.c. supply.
  3. Collect any gas in inverted test tubes over the electrodes. Observe each electrode.
  4. Test the gases.

RP · Temperature changes
  1. Measure 30 cm³ of hydrochloric acid into a polystyrene cup, standing in a beaker so it doesn't tip.
  2. Record the starting temperature with a thermometer.
  3. Add 5 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution, stir, put the lid on and record the highest temperature.
  4. Repeat with 10, 15, 20… cm³ of sodium hydroxide.

Unfamiliar context? Don't panic

Lots of marks are on things you haven't been taught directly: an element you've never met, a new compound, a new reaction. They're always testing a topic you do know.

  1. Find the topic: an element at the bottom of Group 1 = group trends. A substance with a weird melting point = structure and bonding. A new equation = moles and ratios.
  2. Use the pattern: if it's in the same group, it behaves like the others, just more or less reactive.
  3. Use the data given. Quote numbers from the table. The examiner put them there for you.
  4. "Predict" and "suggest" mean apply what you know to a new situation: give a plausible, scientific answer that fits the information. Always write something.

Use the marks as a guide. A 3-mark question usually needs three creditworthy points, steps or linked ideas. Check the command word and develop your answer to match.

"Explain why X has a high/low melting point"
  1. Name the structure (giant ionic, simple molecular, giant covalent, metallic).
  2. Name what holds it together (electrostatic forces between ions / covalent bonds / intermolecular forces).
  3. Say whether they're strong or weak.
  4. Link to energy: a lot / little energy needed to overcome them.
Example
Methane is a simple molecule with weak intermolecular forces between molecules, so little energy is needed to overcome them.
Mark killer: saying covalent bonds break when a simple molecule melts or boils. That gets 0.
"Explain why X conducts (or doesn't)"
  1. Name the charged particle: ions or delocalised electrons.
  2. Say they're free to move.
  3. Say they carry charge (current) through the substance.
Example
Molten NaCl conducts because the ions are free to move and carry charge. Solid NaCl doesn't because the ions are fixed in the lattice.
Mark killer: "electrons move" for an ionic compound. In ionic compounds it's always the ions.
Group 1 and Group 7 reactivity

Group 1, more reactive going down:

  1. More shells, so outer electron is further from the nucleus.
  2. Weaker attraction to the nucleus (more shielding).
  3. Outer electron is lost more easily.

Group 7, less reactive going down: same first two points, but they need to gain an electron, so it gets harder.

Mark killer: "the atom is bigger so it's more reactive". You need the attraction and losing/gaining an electron.
Mole calculations from an equation
  1. Write the Mr of what you know and what you want.
  2. Moles of known = mass ÷ Mr.
  3. Use the balancing numbers for the ratio.
  4. Mass of unknown = moles × Mr.
Example
4.8 g Mg in Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Moles Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.2. Ratio 1:1, so 0.2 mol MgCl₂. Mass = 0.2 × 95 = 19 g.

Always show working. Even with the wrong final answer you can get 2–3 marks out of 4.

Concentration and unit traps
  1. Convert cm³ to dm³: ÷ 1000.
  2. Concentration = mass ÷ volume.
  3. Rearrange when needed: mass = concentration × volume.
Example
5.0 g in 200 cm³ → 0.200 dm³ → 5.0 ÷ 0.200 = 25 g/dm³.
Bond energy calculations
  1. Draw out or list every bond in the reactants. Add them up (bonds broken).
  2. Do the same for the products (bonds made). Remember the big numbers: 2HCl = 2 H–Cl bonds.
  3. Energy change = broken − made.
  4. Negative means exothermic, positive means endothermic.
6-mark method questions

Marked in levels, not points. To get Level 3 (5–6 marks) you need a complete method in a logical order that someone else could follow.

  1. Write numbered steps, one action per step.
  2. Name equipment (beaker, filter funnel, evaporating basin, Bunsen).
  3. Say why for the key steps ("add copper oxide until in excess so all the acid reacts").
  4. Finish the job: get to dry crystals or a pure product.

Learn the two classics: salt from rock salt and copper sulfate from copper oxide. Both are in the paper.

Electrolysis questions
  1. Metal (or hydrogen) goes to the cathode (−). Non-metal to the anode (+).
  2. Cathode: ions gain electrons = reduction. Anode: ions lose electrons = oxidation.
  3. Half equations: count charge. Pb²⁺ needs 2e⁻. Two Br⁻ make Br₂ and give 2e⁻.
  4. Must be molten or dissolved so ions are free to move.
