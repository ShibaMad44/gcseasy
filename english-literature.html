An Inspector Calls · J.B. Priestley

Two dates, one message

Set: spring 1912Written: 1945
Edwardian England: rigid class system, huge gap between rich and poorThe end of WW2; Britain had survived two world wars in 30 years
No welfare state: no unemployment pay, no NHS. Working class women like Eva had no safety netLabour won a landslide in July 1945, promising a welfare state; the NHS followed in 1948
Women couldn't vote; middle class women didn't work for payWomen had done vital war work; attitudes were changing
Just before the Titanic sank (April 1912) and WW1 (1914)The audience knows Birling is wrong about the Titanic and war: dramatic irony

Priestley sets the play in 1912 so his 1945 audience can see where selfishness led (two wars) and choose a better, fairer society now.

Priestley's beliefs

  • A socialist: he believed society should share wealth and look after the vulnerable. He co-founded the Common Wealth Party (1942).
  • His wartime BBC radio talks, the Postscripts (1940), were so popular and left-wing they were taken off air.
  • He'd seen poverty growing up in Bradford and fought in WW1. Those experiences shaped the Inspector's warning of 'fire and blood and anguish'.

Key ideas to know

  • Capitalism: individuals and businesses keep profit for themselves (Birling). Socialism: wealth and responsibility are shared (the Inspector).
  • Collective responsibility: we are all responsible for each other.
  • Class hierarchy: upper (Gerald, the Crofts), middle (the Birlings), working (Eva, Edna).
  • Gender: men had power and money; women were expected to marry and obey.

Form and structure

  • A well-made play and a detective thriller: one confession after another, each act ending on a cliffhanger.
  • A morality play: the Inspector is like a moral messenger judging sins (pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony).
  • Unity of time and place: one evening, one dining room, in real time. The family can't escape.
  • Cyclical ending: the final phone call means the whole thing will happen again, this time with a real inspector.
  • Name: Inspector Goole sounds like 'ghoul', a ghost. Is he real? Priestley leaves it open.

Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde · R.L. Stevenson

Victorian London (published 1886)

  • Respectability and reputation mattered above everything for middle class gentlemen. Scandal could ruin a career, so private vices were hidden: the 'double life'.
  • Repression: strict moral codes meant desires were pushed down, not expressed. Stevenson suggests this makes them stronger and more dangerous.
  • London as a divided city: wealthy, respectable squares beside poor, crime-ridden areas like Soho. Jekyll's house is the same: grand front, 'blistered' back door.
  • Gentlemen's code: never gossip, never betray a friend's secrets. That's why Utterson, Enfield and Lanyon stay silent.

Science and religion

  • Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) suggested humans evolved from apes. Many feared people could degenerate back into primitive, animal states: hence Hyde's 'ape-like fury' and 'troglodytic' look.
  • Science was advancing fast, and some feared scientists were playing God. Lanyon represents traditional, safe science; Jekyll represents 'transcendental' science that crosses moral lines.
  • Religion still shaped morality. Characters keep invoking God and the devil around Hyde ('Satan's signature', 'God bless me').
  • Physiognomy: the Victorian belief that you could read someone's character in their face. Everyone sees evil in Hyde but can't say why.

Stevenson's life

  • Grew up in Edinburgh, a city split between the elegant New Town and the poor Old Town.
  • Raised in a strict Calvinist household that taught people are born sinful; he rebelled as a young man.
  • Inspired by Deacon Brodie, a respected Edinburgh cabinet-maker by day and burglar by night.
  • Said the idea came to him in a nightmare.

Form and structure

  • Gothic novella: fog, darkness, secrets, the double (doppelgänger). Unusually, the monster comes from inside a respectable man, not from outside.
  • Detective / mystery structure: we follow Utterson, who knows as little as we do.
  • Multiple narratives: the truth is held back until the last two chapters, Lanyon's and Jekyll's letters. This delays the reveal and mirrors the theme of secrecy.
  • Pathetic fallacy: the fog hides the truth, just as characters hide their secrets.

Romeo and Juliet · William Shakespeare

When and where

  • Written about 1595, in the reign of Elizabeth I. Set in Verona, Italy, over about four days in summer.
  • Based on an older poem, Arthur Brooke's The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet (1562). Shakespeare's audience already knew the ending.
  • The Prologue is a sonnet that tells us the "star-crossed lovers" will die, so the play is about how it happens, not whether.

Elizabethan context

  • Patriarchy: fathers controlled daughters. Marriages were often arranged for money and status, and girls could marry young (Juliet is 13). Disobeying a father was a serious sin.
  • Fate and the stars: many people believed in astrology and that life was planned by God or fate.
  • Honour and violence: young men defended their family's honour with swords; duelling was common, though Elizabeth tried to ban it.
  • Religion: suicide was a mortal sin. Friar Laurence is a respected Catholic priest, yet his plans go wrong.
  • Courtly love: a fashion for poetic, unrequited love, which Romeo copies at first with Rosaline.

Form and structure

  • A tragedy: a noble hero with a flaw (Romeo's impulsiveness), a fall, and deaths that bring a lesson (the families make peace).
  • The first half feels like a comedy (love, a party, a secret wedding); Mercutio's death in Act 3 Scene 1 is the turning point.
  • The lovers' first conversation is a shared sonnet (Act 1 Scene 5): their love is perfect and matched.
  • Time speeds up: the action is rushed into a few days, showing how hasty choices lead to tragedy.
  • Verse for nobles and serious moments; prose for servants and jokes (the Nurse, Mercutio's banter).
  • Light and dark imagery runs through the play: the lovers meet in darkness and their love shines like light.

Useful pairings

  • Internal conflict: Envy, There's a certain Slant of light, Boat Stealing, Honour Killing.
  • War and its victims: Vergissmeinnicht, What Were They Like?, Lament, The Destruction of Sennacherib, Papa-T.
  • Guilt and responsibility: We Lived Happily during the War, Boat Stealing, Partition, Lament.
  • Identity, nation and power: Flag, Colonization in Reverse, Honour Killing, Thirteen.
  • Hope and unity: Songs for the People (contrast it with Flag or Sennacherib).

Methods to look for

  • Voice: first person "I" or "we" (Kaminsky's collective guilt), second person "you" (Femi), dialogue or questions (Agard, Levertov).
  • Form: regular rhyme and rhythm can contrast with violence (Byron's galloping rhythm, Douglas's tight quatrains); free verse feels raw (Kaminsky, Femi).
  • Imagery: nature (Lamb, Wordsworth, Clarke), light and dark (Dickinson), religious imagery (Byron, Harper, Dickinson).
  • Structure: turning points (Wordsworth's mountain, Douglas's photograph), repetition and lists (Clarke, Harper), endings that echo or shock.
How to write a top paragraph: What, How, Why
  1. What: make a point that answers the question, with a short embedded quote. "Priestley presents Birling as selfish when he says a man must 'look after himself and his own'."
  2. How: zoom in on a word or technique. "The possessive 'his own' shows he only cares for his family, never society."
  3. Why: the writer's purpose and context. "Priestley, writing in 1945, wants the audience to reject this capitalist selfishness and vote for a fairer society."

Always write about the writer ("Priestley presents…", "Stevenson suggests…"), not the characters as if they're real people.

Part (a): comparing with the unseen extract
  • Read the unseen extract twice. Underline 3–4 short quotes linked to the question.
  • Every paragraph should mention both extracts. Use comparative connectives: similarly, likewise, whereas, in contrast, however.
  • Compare methods too: stage directions, dialogue, pauses, how power shifts.
  • Don't spend long on context here; focus on language and drama.
Part (b) and Section B essays
  • Open with a thesis: one or two sentences that answer the question directly, including the writer's purpose.
  • Use 3–4 paragraphs, each with a quote you analyse closely.
  • Move across the text: beginning, middle, end, so you show the whole play or novella.
  • Weave context in where it explains the writer's purpose; don't bolt on a context paragraph.
  • For Jekyll and Hyde extract questions, start with the extract (about a third of your answer), then move across the novella.
  • Check SPaG in Section B: it's worth 4 marks.
