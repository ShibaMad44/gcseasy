P1En

Energy

Energy stores and transfers

Energy is stored in stores: kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal, chemical, magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear. It moves between stores in four ways: mechanically (a force does work), electrically (a current does work), by heating, and by radiation (light, sound).

A system is an object or group of objects. When a system changes, energy moves between stores. Example: a ball thrown upwards: kinetic store → gravitational potential store.

StoreEquationUnits
KineticEₖ = ½mv²J, kg, m/s
Gravitational potentialEₚ = mghJ, kg, N/kg, m
Elastic potentialEₑ = ½ke²J, N/m, m (extension)
Thermal (heating)ΔE = mcΔθJ, kg, J/kg °C, °C

Specific heat capacity (c): the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. Water has a high c (4200 J/kg °C), so it heats up and cools down slowly.

Watch out: in Eₖ = ½mv², only the speed is squared. Square v first, then multiply by m, then halve.

Power and efficiency

Power is the rate of energy transfer, or the rate of doing work. 1 watt = 1 joule per second.

P = E ÷ t  |  P = W ÷ t
efficiency = useful output energy ÷ total input energy  (or useful power out ÷ total power in)

Efficiency is a decimal or a percentage (× 100). It can never be more than 1 (100%). Wasted energy is usually dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings.

Reducing wasted energy

  • Lubrication reduces friction between moving parts.
  • Thermal insulation (loft insulation, cavity walls, double glazing) reduces heat loss. Materials with a low thermal conductivity transfer energy more slowly.
  • Thicker walls and walls made of lower thermal conductivity material mean a slower rate of cooling for a building.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

Required practical: "Describe how to find the specific heat capacity of a metal block."
  1. Measure the mass of the block on a balance.
  2. Wrap it in an insulating layer to reduce energy lost to the surroundings.
  3. Put an immersion heater and a thermometer into the holes in the block (a drop of water in the thermometer hole improves contact).
  4. Connect the heater to a power supply with an ammeter and voltmeter, or a joulemeter. Record the start temperature.
  5. Switch on for 10 minutes, recording the temperature every minute. Energy = V × I × t.
  6. Calculate c = ΔE ÷ (m × Δθ). Your value is likely too high, because some energy heats the surroundings rather than the block.
Required practical: "Describe how to compare materials as thermal insulators." Wrap identical beakers in different materials of the same thickness. Fill each with the same volume of hot water from a kettle and put on a lid. Record the starting temperature and the temperature every 2 minutes for 20 minutes. The best insulator has the smallest temperature drop. Control: water volume, start temperature, thickness, room temperature.
"Evaluate the use of different energy resources" (e.g. wind vs gas). Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas): reliable, can meet demand quickly, but non-renewable; release CO₂ (climate change) and sulfur dioxide (acid rain). Nuclear: reliable, no CO₂, but radioactive waste and expensive to build or decommission. Wind and solar: renewable, no CO₂ in use, but unreliable (no wind, at night) and use lots of land. Hydroelectric: reliable and quick to respond, but floods valleys. Tidal: predictable but harms habitats and has few sites. Give a judgement, e.g. "wind should be used alongside a reliable backup."
"Explain ways of reducing unwanted energy transfers in a house." Loft insulation and cavity wall insulation trap air, a poor conductor, which reduces conduction and convection. Double glazing puts a gap of air or vacuum between panes. Thicker walls with lower thermal conductivity reduce the rate of energy transfer. Draught excluders reduce convection. In machines, lubrication reduces friction.

Electricity

Current, charge and potential difference

  • Current (I, amps): the rate of flow of charge. Q = It (charge in coulombs, time in seconds).
  • Potential difference (V, volts): the energy transferred per unit charge. Measured with a voltmeter in parallel.
  • Resistance (R, ohms Ω): how much a component opposes the current. V = IR
  • Ammeters go in series; voltmeters go in parallel.

Mains electricity

  • UK mains is a.c. at 230 V and 50 Hz. Batteries give d.c. (one direction).
  • Live (brown): carries the 230 V. Neutral (blue): completes the circuit, around 0 V. Earth (green and yellow): safety wire, only carries current if there's a fault.
  • Touching the live wire is dangerous even if the switch is off: your body is at 0 V, so there's a large p.d. and a current flows through you.

Power and energy in circuits

P = VI  |  P = I²R  |  E = Pt  |  E = QV

The National Grid

A system of cables and transformers linking power stations to consumers. Step-up transformers increase the p.d. (to around 400 000 V) so the current is lower. Lower current means less heating of the cables, so less energy is wasted and transmission is more efficient. Step-down transformers reduce the p.d. to 230 V for homes.

Static electricity Triple

  • Rubbing two insulators together transfers electrons from one to the other. The one that gains electrons becomes negative; the one that loses them becomes positive.
  • Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. A charged object can attract small uncharged objects.
  • If enough charge builds up, the p.d. gets high enough for a spark to jump across the gap.
  • A charged object creates an electric field around it. The field is strongest close to the object. Field lines point away from positive and towards negative charges.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

Required practical: "Describe how to investigate how the length of a wire affects its resistance."
  1. Tape a wire along a metre ruler and connect it in series with an ammeter and a cell, using a crocodile clip at 0 cm and another at a set length.
  2. Connect a voltmeter in parallel across the length of wire being tested.
  3. Start at 10 cm, record V and I, and calculate R = V ÷ I.
  4. Repeat in 10 cm steps up to 100 cm.
  5. Use a low current and switch off between readings so the wire doesn't heat up (heat increases resistance).
  6. Plot R against length: a straight line through the origin shows that resistance is directly proportional to length.
Required practical: "Describe how to find the I–V characteristic of a component." Set up a circuit with the component, an ammeter in series, a voltmeter in parallel and a variable resistor. Change the resistor to vary the current and record V and I. Reverse the supply to get negative values. Plot I against V. A resistor gives a straight line (ohmic, at constant temperature). A filament lamp curves, because as it heats up, ions vibrate more and resistance increases. A diode only lets current flow one way.
"Compare series and parallel circuits." Series: the same current everywhere. The potential difference is shared between components. Total resistance = R₁ + R₂. If one bulb breaks, they all go out. Parallel: the same p.d. across each branch. The current splits between branches. Total resistance is less than the smallest resistor. Each branch can be switched separately. Houses are wired in parallel.
"Explain how the National Grid transfers energy efficiently." Power stations produce electricity. A step-up transformer increases the p.d. to about 400 000 V, which reduces the current for the same power (P = VI). Lower current means less heating of the cables (P = I²R), so less energy is wasted. Near homes, step-down transformers reduce the p.d. to a safe 230 V.
"Explain the dangers of the live wire and the role of the earth wire and fuse." The live wire (brown) is at 230 V, so touching it lets current flow through you to earth: an electric shock. The neutral (blue) is around 0 V. The earth wire (green/yellow) connects metal casings to the ground. If the live wire touches the casing, a large current flows to earth, which melts the fuse and breaks the circuit, so the appliance is safe.
P3Pm

Particle model of matter

Density

ρ = m ÷ V   (kg/m³ = kg ÷ m³)

Solids are usually densest (particles close together in a regular pattern), liquids slightly less, gases much less dense (particles far apart).

StateArrangementMovement
SolidClose together, regular patternVibrate in fixed positions
LiquidClose together, randomMove past each other
GasFar apart, randomMove quickly in all directions

Internal energy and changes of state

  • Internal energy = total kinetic + potential energy of all the particles in a system.
  • Heating raises the temperature or changes the state, not both at once.
  • Changes of state are physical changes: mass is conserved and the change can be reversed.
  • On a heating curve the flat parts are changes of state: energy is breaking the bonds between particles (increasing potential energy), so the temperature doesn't rise.

Specific latent heat

E = mL

Specific latent heat (L): the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance with no change in temperature. Latent heat of fusion: solid ↔ liquid. Latent heat of vaporisation: liquid ↔ gas.

Don't mix them up: specific heat capacity is about changing temperature (ΔE = mcΔθ). Specific latent heat is about changing state (E = mL).

Gas pressure

Gas particles move randomly and collide with the container walls, exerting a force. Pressure = force per unit area. Heating a gas at constant volume gives particles more kinetic energy, so they hit the walls harder and more often, and the pressure increases.

Triple At constant temperature, pV = constant. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles (particles hit the walls more often). HT Doing work on a gas (e.g. a bike pump) transfers energy to it, so its temperature rises.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

Required practical: "Describe how to find the density of an irregular object."
  1. Measure its mass on a top-pan balance.
  2. Fill a eureka (displacement) can with water until it drips out of the spout, and wait until it stops.
  3. Put a measuring cylinder under the spout and gently lower the object in.
  4. The volume of water displaced equals the volume of the object.
  5. Calculate density = mass ÷ volume.
  6. For a regular block, measure length × width × height with a ruler instead. For a liquid, measure the mass of a known volume.
"Explain changes of state using the particle model." When a substance is heated, its particles gain internal energy (kinetic and potential). Melting: particles vibrate enough to overcome some forces and move past each other. Boiling: particles have enough energy to break away completely. During a change of state, the temperature stays constant because energy is used to break bonds (latent heat), not to raise kinetic energy. Changes of state are physical and reversible, and mass is conserved.
"Explain why the pressure of a gas increases when it's heated in a sealed container." Heating gives the gas particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster. They hit the container walls more often and with more force. Pressure = force ÷ area, so the pressure increases. (For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, compressing it into a smaller volume also increases collisions, so pV = constant.)
P4At

Atomic structure and radiation

The atom

  • The atom has a radius of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. The nucleus is about 10 000 times smaller, but contains almost all the mass.
  • Protons (+1) and neutrons (0) are in the nucleus. Electrons (−1) are in energy levels (shells). Electrons move to a higher level by absorbing EM radiation and drop back by emitting it.
  • Isotopes: same number of protons, different number of neutrons. ¹⁴₆C has 6 protons and 8 neutrons.

Model of the atom over time

  1. Dalton: atoms are tiny solid spheres that can't be divided.
  2. Thomson (plum pudding): found the electron. A ball of positive charge with negative electrons in it.
  3. Rutherford (alpha scattering): most alpha particles went straight through gold foil (atom is mostly empty space); a few bounced back (tiny, dense, positive nucleus). Gave the nuclear model.
  4. Bohr: electrons orbit the nucleus at fixed distances (energy levels). Agreed with experiments.
  5. Chadwick: discovered the neutron, about 20 years later.

Decay equations

Alpha: ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He  (mass − 4, atomic − 2)
Beta: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e  (mass same, atomic + 1)

Gamma emission doesn't change the mass or atomic number. Top numbers and bottom numbers must balance on both sides.

Half-life and activity

  • Radioactive decay is random: you can't predict which nucleus will decay next.
  • Activity: the rate at which a source decays, in becquerels (Bq). Count rate is what a Geiger-Müller tube detects.
  • Half-life: the time for the number of unstable nuclei (or the activity) to halve.
  • Example: 800 Bq, half-life 2 days. After 6 days (3 half-lives): 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 Bq.
  • HT Net decline as a ratio: after 3 half-lives, the activity is ⅛ of the start.

Contamination and irradiation

Irradiation: being exposed to radiation from an outside source. It stops when you move away; you don't become radioactive. Contamination: radioactive atoms get onto or into something. It's more dangerous because the exposure carries on. Alpha is most dangerous inside the body (highly ionising); outside the body, gamma and beta are more dangerous because they can reach your organs.

Peer review matters: findings on radiation's effects are checked by other scientists before being accepted.

Background radiation and uses Triple

  • Background radiation comes from natural sources (radon gas, rocks, cosmic rays) and man-made sources (nuclear weapons testing fallout, nuclear accidents). Dose is measured in sieverts (Sv); usually millisieverts (mSv).
  • Medical uses: tracers (gamma emitters with short half-lives, so they don't stay active in the body) and radiotherapy (gamma beams or implants to kill cancer cells).
  • Choose a source by its half-life and penetration: a tracer must reach a detector outside the body, so it's gamma.

Fission and fusion Triple

  • Fission: a large unstable nucleus (uranium-235 or plutonium-239) absorbs a neutron, then splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing 2 or 3 neutrons, gamma rays and lots of energy. The neutrons can cause more fissions: a chain reaction. In a reactor, control rods absorb neutrons to keep it steady.
  • Fusion: two light nuclei (e.g. hydrogen) join to make a heavier nucleus. Some mass is converted to energy. It powers the Sun. It needs extremely high temperature and pressure, so it's hard to do on Earth.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

"Describe how the alpha scattering experiment changed the model of the atom." Before: the plum pudding model (a ball of positive charge with electrons in it). Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at thin gold foil. Most went straight through, so the atom is mostly empty space. Some were deflected, so the centre is positively charged. A very few bounced back, so most of the mass is concentrated in a tiny nucleus. This gave the nuclear model. Bohr then added electrons in shells, and Chadwick found the neutron.

P5Fo

Forces

Forces and their interactions

  • Scalar: size only (speed, distance, mass, time, energy). Vector: size and direction (velocity, displacement, force, acceleration, momentum).
  • Contact forces: friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force. Non-contact forces: gravitational, electrostatic, magnetic.
  • Weight = mass × gravitational field strength: W = mg (N, kg, N/kg). Weight acts at the centre of mass. Measured with a newtonmeter.
  • The resultant force is the single force with the same effect as all the forces acting together.
  • Work done = force × distance moved along the line of the force: W = Fs. 1 joule = 1 newton-metre. Work against friction heats things up.

Springs (Hooke's law)

  • F = ke (force N, spring constant N/m, extension m), up to the limit of proportionality.
  • Elastic deformation: returns to its shape. Inelastic: doesn't.
  • Elastic potential energy: Eₑ = ½ke².
  • Required practical: hang masses on a spring, measure the extension with a ruler (eye level, fixed pointer), plot force against extension. A straight line through the origin shows proportionality; the gradient is k.

Motion

  • Speed = distance ÷ time (s = vt). Typical speeds: walking 1.5 m/s, running 3 m/s, cycling 6 m/s, sound in air 330 m/s.
  • Velocity is speed in a given direction. An object moving in a circle at constant speed has a changing velocity.
  • Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time: a = Δv ÷ t (m/s²). And v² − u² = 2as.
  • Distance–time graph: gradient = speed. Velocity–time graph: gradient = acceleration; HT area under the graph = distance travelled.
  • An object falling through a fluid reaches terminal velocity when air resistance equals its weight (resultant force zero).

Newton's laws

  • First law: if the resultant force is zero, a stationary object stays still and a moving object keeps the same velocity. HT Inertia: the tendency to stay at rest or keep moving.
  • Second law: F = ma. Acceleration is proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to mass.
  • Third law: when two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other.
  • Required practical: pull a trolley with a string over a pulley with hanging masses; measure acceleration with light gates. Vary the force (move masses from the trolley to the hanger, keeping total mass constant) or vary the mass.

Stopping distances

  • Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.
  • Thinking distance increases with speed, tiredness, drugs, alcohol and distractions (slower reaction time, typically 0.2–0.9 s).
  • Braking distance increases with speed, wet or icy roads, worn brakes or tyres, and a heavier vehicle.
  • When brakes are applied, friction does work, transferring kinetic energy to thermal energy in the brakes. Large decelerations can overheat brakes or make the vehicle skid.

Momentum HT

  • Momentum p = mv (kg m/s). In a closed system, total momentum before = total momentum after (conservation of momentum).
  • Triple Force = change in momentum ÷ time. Airbags, seat belts and crumple zones increase the time of a collision, so they reduce the force.

Moments and pressure Triple

  • Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot (M = Fd). Balanced when clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments. Levers and gears multiply the effect of a force.
  • Pressure in a fluid = force ÷ area; in a liquid p = hρg, so pressure increases with depth. Upthrust makes objects float if it's at least as big as their weight. Atmospheric pressure falls with height.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

Required practical: "Investigate how the force on a trolley affects its acceleration."
  1. Attach a string to a trolley, run it over a pulley at the end of the bench and hang a mass holder on it.
  2. Set up light gates (or a ticker timer) to measure the acceleration.
  3. Release the trolley and record the acceleration. Force = weight of the hanging masses (W = mg).
  4. Move one mass at a time from the trolley to the hanger, so the total mass stays constant while the force increases.
  5. Repeat each force and calculate the mean. Plot acceleration against force: a straight line shows a ∝ F.
"Explain the factors that affect a car's stopping distance." Stopping distance = thinking + braking distance. Thinking distance is longer if the driver is tired, has drunk alcohol, taken drugs or is distracted, because their reaction time is longer, and it increases with speed. Braking distance is longer at higher speeds, on wet or icy roads (less friction), with worn tyres or brakes, and with a heavier car. Braking turns kinetic energy into heat in the brakes.
"Describe the motion of a skydiver from jumping to landing." At first, weight is bigger than air resistance, so the skydiver accelerates downwards. As speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant force and acceleration decrease. When air resistance equals weight, the resultant force is zero: terminal velocity. Opening the parachute increases surface area and air resistance, so they decelerate until a new, lower terminal velocity is reached.
Required practical: "Investigate the extension of a spring." Clamp a spring next to a vertical ruler and attach a pointer. Record the unstretched length. Add 1 N masses one at a time and record the length each time (at eye level). Extension = length − original length. Plot force against extension. Stop before the limit of proportionality and watch for the spring flicking.
P6Wa

Waves

Wave basics

  • Waves transfer energy, not matter.
  • Transverse: oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (e.g. water ripples, all electromagnetic waves). Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer, with compressions and rarefactions (e.g. sound).
  • Amplitude: maximum displacement from the rest position. Wavelength: distance from one point to the same point on the next wave. Frequency: waves passing a point per second (Hz). Period T = 1 ÷ f.
  • Wave speed = frequency × wavelength: v = fλ.
  • Required practical: a ripple tank (measure the wavelength of several waves with a ruler against a lamp's shadow; count waves per second; v = fλ) or waves on a string with a vibration generator.

Electromagnetic waves

  • All EM waves are transverse and travel at the same speed in a vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s). In order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength): radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.
  • Uses: radio (TV and radio), microwaves (satellite communications, cooking), infrared (heaters, cooking, thermal cameras), visible (fibre optics), ultraviolet (energy-efficient lamps, sun tanning), X-rays and gamma (medical imaging and treatments).
  • Dangers: UV ages skin and increases the risk of skin cancer. X-rays and gamma are ionising: they can cause gene mutations and cancer.
  • Refraction: when a wave changes speed as it crosses a boundary between materials, it changes direction (unless it hits along the normal).
  • Required practical: infrared radiation using a Leslie cube (hot water inside; measure with an IR detector at the same distance from each face). Matt black emits the most; shiny silver the least.

Lenses, light and sound Triple

  • Convex lenses bring light to a focus (real or virtual images); concave lenses spread it out (virtual images). Magnification = image height ÷ object height.
  • An object looks a colour because it reflects that colour and absorbs the others. All bodies emit infrared; hotter bodies emit more and shorter wavelengths.
  • Ultrasound (above 20 000 Hz) is used for scans and echo sounding. Seismic waves: P-waves (longitudinal, through solids and liquids) and S-waves (transverse, solids only); this shows the outer core is liquid.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

Required practical: "Measure the speed of water waves in a ripple tank." Set up the ripple tank with a vibrating bar and a lamp above to cast shadows of the waves onto paper. Measure across several waves (e.g. 10) with a ruler and divide to get one wavelength. Count how many waves pass a point in 10 seconds and divide by 10 to get frequency. Calculate speed with v = fλ. A strobe or a photo helps freeze the waves.
"Compare the uses and dangers of different EM waves." Low-frequency waves (radio, microwaves) are used for communication and are low risk. Infrared heats and cooks. UV is useful for lamps and tanning but damages skin and causes skin cancer. X-rays and gamma are used in medicine for images and treating cancer, but they're ionising, so they can mutate DNA; staff limit exposure with shielding.
P7Ma

Magnetism and electromagnetism

Magnets

  • Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Magnetic forces are non-contact.
  • A permanent magnet has its own field. An induced magnet becomes magnetic only in a field and always attracts.
  • Magnetic materials: iron, steel, cobalt, nickel.
  • Field lines go from north to south; the field is strongest where lines are closest (at the poles). A compass needle points along the field. The Earth has a magnetic field (its core acts like a magnet).

Electromagnetism

  • A current in a wire creates a magnetic field in circles around it (direction from the right-hand grip rule). Stronger with more current and closer to the wire.
  • A solenoid (coil) has a field like a bar magnet; adding an iron core makes an electromagnet. Stronger with more turns, more current, or an iron core. It can be switched off.
  • Uses: scrapyard cranes, electric bells, relays, door locks.
  • HT Motor effect: a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force. F = BIl (N, tesla, A, m). Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule (First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion).
  • HT In an electric motor, forces on the coil's two sides act in opposite directions, so it rotates; a split-ring commutator reverses the current every half turn to keep it spinning.

Generators and transformers Triple

  • Loudspeakers use the motor effect to vibrate a cone.
  • HT Generator effect: moving a wire in a magnetic field (or changing the field) induces a potential difference. Alternators make a.c.; dynamos make d.c.
  • HT Transformers: Vp ÷ Vs = np ÷ ns, and Vp × Ip = Vs × Is (if 100% efficient). Step-up increases p.d.

In depth: likely 6-mark questions

"Describe how to make an electromagnet stronger, and how to test its strength." Increase the current, add more turns to the coil, or use an iron core. Test by counting how many paper clips it can pick up. Keep everything else the same and repeat for reliability.
HT "Explain how an electric motor works." A coil carrying a current sits in a magnetic field. By the motor effect, each side of the coil feels a force (Fleming's left-hand rule); the forces are in opposite directions, so the coil turns. A split-ring commutator reverses the current every half turn, so the forces keep turning the coil the same way. Faster with more current, a stronger field or more turns.
P8Sp

Space physics Triple

The solar system and stars Triple

  • Our solar system: one star (the Sun), eight planets, dwarf planets, and natural satellites (moons). It's part of the Milky Way galaxy.
  • Life cycle of a star like the Sun: nebula (dust and gas) → protostar → main sequence star (fusion balances gravity) → red giant → white dwarf → black dwarf. Much bigger stars: red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole.
  • Fusion in stars makes all the elements up to iron; heavier elements form in supernovae.
  • Gravity provides the force for circular orbits. HT Faster orbits need a smaller radius.
  • Red-shift: light from distant galaxies is shifted to longer wavelengths, and more for further galaxies, so the universe is expanding. This supports the Big Bang theory. Dark matter and dark energy are still unexplained.

Required practicals are worth around 15% of the marks, and 6-mark "describe a method" questions usually come from them. For each one, know the method, the variables and the safety points.

  1. Measure the mass of the metal block (in kg) on a balance.
  2. Wrap the block in insulation to reduce energy lost to the surroundings.
  3. Put the heater in the large hole and a thermometer in the small hole (add a drop of water for good thermal contact).
  4. Connect the heater to a power supply with an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel (or use a joulemeter).
  5. Record the starting temperature, switch on and start the stopwatch.
  6. Record the temperature, current and p.d. every minute for 10 minutes.
  7. Calculate the energy transferred: E = Pt = VIt. Plot temperature against energy; the gradient = 1 ÷ (mc), so c = 1 ÷ (gradient × m).
Independent
Energy transferred (or time)
Dependent
Temperature rise
Control
Mass of block, insulation, power of heater
Error
Energy lost to the surroundings makes c come out too high. Insulate the block.
Safety
The heater and block get hot: don't touch, let them cool.
RP · Thermal insulation Triple
  1. Put a small beaker inside a larger beaker. Fill the small one with a set volume of hot water from a kettle.
  2. Pack the gap with an insulating material (e.g. bubble wrap, cotton wool, newspaper) and put on a lid.
  3. Record the temperature every 2 minutes for 20 minutes with a thermometer.
  4. Repeat with different materials, and with different thicknesses of the same material.
  5. Compare the temperature drop: the best insulator gives the smallest drop.
Independent
Type (or thickness) of insulating material
Dependent
Temperature drop over 20 minutes
Control
Volume and starting temperature of the water, time, beaker size, lid
Safety
Hot water: handle beakers carefully.
RP · Resistance of a wire
  1. Tape a length of wire (e.g. constantan) to a metre ruler. Connect a crocodile clip at 0 cm.
  2. Connect a battery, ammeter in series, and a voltmeter across the length of wire being tested.
  3. Put the second crocodile clip at 10 cm. Record the current and p.d., then switch off.
  4. Repeat for 20, 30 … 100 cm. Calculate R = V ÷ I for each length.
  5. Plot resistance against length: a straight line through the origin shows R is directly proportional to length.
Independent
Length of wire
Dependent
Resistance
Control
Wire material, diameter, temperature, p.d. of supply
Why low current / switch off?
So the wire doesn't heat up; heating would increase its resistance.
RP · I–V characteristics
  1. Set up a circuit with a power supply, variable resistor, ammeter in series and voltmeter across the component.
  2. Use the variable resistor to change the p.d. Record current and p.d. for several values.
  3. Swap the power supply connections and repeat to get negative values.
  4. Do this for a fixed resistor, a filament lamp and a diode. Plot current against p.d.
Expect
Resistor: straight line through the origin. Lamp: S-curve. Diode: current in one direction only.
Safety
The lamp gets hot. Keep the current low for the resistor so it doesn't heat up.
RP · Density
  1. Regular object: measure the mass on a balance. Measure length, width and height with a ruler; volume = l × w × h. Density = m ÷ V.
  2. Irregular object: measure the mass. Fill a eureka (displacement) can to the spout. Lower the object in and collect the water that overflows in a measuring cylinder. That volume = the object's volume.
  3. Liquid: measure the mass of an empty measuring cylinder, add a known volume of liquid, measure again. Mass of liquid ÷ volume.
Accuracy
Read the measuring cylinder at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus. Repeat and take a mean.
Units
1 cm³ = 1 ml. g/cm³ × 1000 = kg/m³.

How to get full marks on a calculation
  1. Write the equation in symbols. This alone can earn a mark.
  2. Convert units first: kJ → J (× 1000), minutes → seconds (× 60), g → kg (÷ 1000), cm → m (÷ 100).
  3. Substitute the numbers, then rearrange if needed.
  4. Calculate, then give the unit and round as asked (significant figures or decimal places).

Command words
  • State / give / name: a short answer, no explanation.
  • Describe: say what happens (e.g. what a graph shows), with data if there's a graph.
  • Explain: say what happens and why. Use "because" and "so".
  • Calculate: show working and give a number with a unit.
  • Determine: use the data or graph given.
  • Evaluate: give pros and cons, then a justified conclusion.
  • Suggest: apply what you know to a new situation. There may be more than one right answer.
6-mark questions
  • Most come from a required practical ("Describe a method…") or an evaluation.
  • Methods: write numbered steps in order, name the equipment, say what you measure and how you get the final value (e.g. calculate R = V ÷ I, plot a graph).
  • Include one control variable and one way to make it accurate or safe.
  • Evaluations: give points on both sides, then a conclusion that answers the question.
Unseen contexts: spot the topic
  • Anything moving, falling or stretching → P1 energy stores and equations.
  • "Heated", "temperature rise" → ΔE = mcΔθ. "Melts", "boils", "no temperature change" → E = mL.
  • Anything with a battery, plug or cable → P2. Plugs and safety → live/neutral/earth.
  • Medical tracers, smoke alarms, space probes → P4: pick radiation by penetration and half-life.
