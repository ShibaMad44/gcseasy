Tectonic hazards

Natural hazards and risk

  • Natural hazard: an extreme natural event that could harm people or damage property. It's only a hazard if people live there. A natural disaster is one that has already happened.
  • Three types: atmospheric (weather), geological (land moving), flooding (water).
  • Hazard risk = the chance of being affected. It depends on location, wealth (HICs afford protection and monitoring but have more valuable property), education (e.g. earthquake drills in Japanese schools), urbanisation (more people and tall buildings close together) and climate change (more sea above 26 °C).

Plate tectonics

  • Earth: core (hottest), mantle (magma), crust (solid rock), split into tectonic plates. Continental plates are thicker and less dense; oceanic plates are thinner and denser.
  • Plates move because of convection currents in the mantle: magma near the core heats, rises, spreads under the crust, cools and sinks. Higher grades: also ridge push and slab pull (gravity).
  • Earthquakes and volcanoes are in belts along plate margins, e.g. the Pacific Ring of Fire and the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Some aren't, like Hawaii.

Effects and responses

  • Primary effects are caused by the hazard itself (ground shaking: deaths, collapsed buildings). Secondary effects are caused by the primary effects (tsunami, landslides, fires, homelessness, disease).
  • Immediate responses: hours to weeks, temporary (rescue, aid, shelters). Long-term responses: months to years, permanent (rebuilding, new laws, warning systems).

Why people live with the risk

  • Geothermal energy is cheap (65% of Iceland's energy).
  • Fertile volcanic soil for farming (olives around Mt Vesuvius).
  • Minerals to mine (copper at Mt St Helens).
  • Tourism (Hawaii's tourism worth over $15 billion).
  • Family and friends, lack of money to move, or the risk feels managed.

In depth: how volcanoes form

At a destructive margin (e.g. the Nazca and South American plates, the Andes):

  1. A dense oceanic plate moves towards a less dense continental plate.
  2. The oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle: this is subduction. The area where it sinks is the subduction zone.
  3. Friction and the heat of the mantle make the subducting plate melt into magma. Water carried down with the plate lowers the melting point.
  4. The new magma is less dense than the rock around it, so it rises through cracks in the continental crust.
  5. Pressure builds in a magma chamber under the surface. The magma is thick and sticky (viscous) and full of gas.
  6. When the pressure is too great, it erupts explosively, forming a steep-sided composite volcano made of layers of ash and lava.

Hazards: pyroclastic flows (fast, very hot clouds of gas and rock), ash falls, lahars (mudflows of ash and water), lava bombs and toxic gases.

At a constructive margin (e.g. Iceland on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge):

  1. Two plates move apart, pulled by convection currents (and slab pull elsewhere on the plates).
  2. The pressure drops in the gap, so mantle rock melts and magma rises to fill it.
  3. The magma is runny (low viscosity) with little gas, so eruptions are gentle and effusive.
  4. Lava flows a long way before cooling, building wide, gently sloping shield volcanoes and new oceanic crust.

In depth: how earthquakes happen

  1. Plates try to move past, towards or away from each other, but friction makes them stick.
  2. Stress (pressure) builds up in the rocks over years or centuries.
  3. Eventually the stress is greater than the friction and the rocks suddenly slip.
  4. Energy is released at the focus (the point underground) and travels out as seismic waves.
  5. The epicentre is the point on the surface directly above the focus, where shaking is usually strongest.

Strength is measured on the moment magnitude scale; each whole number is about 32 times more energy. Shallow-focus earthquakes cause more damage than deep ones.

Tsunamis form at destructive margins: the sea bed suddenly lifts as the plates slip, pushing up the water above. The wave travels fast across the ocean and grows tall as it reaches shallow water near the coast (e.g. Japan 2011).

In depth: why effects differ between places

  • Wealth: HICs can afford earthquake-proof buildings, warning systems and fast rescue (Japan 2011: ~16 000 deaths from a 9.0 earthquake and tsunami). LICs often can't (Nepal 2015: ~9 000 deaths from a 7.8 earthquake).
  • Building quality and enforcing building codes.
  • Preparedness: drills, education, emergency supplies.
  • Physical factors: magnitude, depth of the focus, distance from the epicentre, ground type, time of day, remoteness (Nepal's mountain villages were cut off by landslides).
  • Population density: more people near the epicentre means more people affected.
9-mark plan: "To what extent do the effects of earthquakes depend on a country's level of development?"
  1. Point 1, development matters: compare Japan (HIC) and Nepal (LIC) with deaths and how fast each responded.
  2. Point 2, why: building codes, warning systems, money for rebuilding.
  3. Point 3, but other factors matter: Japan still had huge losses from the tsunami (secondary effect) and the Fukushima disaster; magnitude, depth and terrain also count.
  4. Judgement: "Development is the most important factor because…, although physical factors decide how big the event is in the first place."

Weather hazards

Global atmospheric circulation

  • Warm air rises → low pressure (e.g. the Equator). Cool air sinks → high pressure (e.g. the poles, 30° N and S).
  • Wind blows from high to low pressure (trade winds blow from 30° to the Equator). Winds curve because of Earth's rotation: the Coriolis effect.
  • Rain: warm water evaporates, air rises, cools, water vapour condenses into clouds, precipitation falls. Wet places are in low pressure belts (the UK, about 55° N); dry places in high pressure belts (hot deserts, 15–30° N and S).

Tropical storms

  • Large, spinning, very low pressure storms. Called hurricanes (Atlantic, NE Pacific), typhoons (NW Pacific), cyclones (Indian Ocean, S Pacific).
  • Form 5–15° north and south of the Equator. Never at the Equator: the Coriolis effect is too weak there.
  • Need: sea at 26 °C or warmer, very low pressure, enough Coriolis effect to spin, low wind shear to stay upright.
  1. Warm sea water (26 °C+) evaporates and rises quickly.
  2. It cools and condenses into tall cumulonimbus clouds.
  3. Storms join into one 480–650 km wide and up to 8 km tall.
  4. The Coriolis effect spins it. Winds of 75 mph or more; over 100 mph at the eye wall.
  5. Cool air sinks in the centre, making the calm, cloudless eye.
  6. The prevailing wind moves it. Over land it loses energy (friction, no warm water).

Climate change could change distribution (further from the Equator), frequency (more often) and intensity (faster winds, more rain), because more sea is above 26 °C for longer. The evidence is mixed.

Reducing risk: satellite thermal maps and tracking (monitoring and prediction; cones of uncertainty), evacuation routes, warnings and survival kits (planning), storm shelters on stilts with shutters, made of concrete (protection).

Extreme weather in the UK

  • Extreme weather is unseasonal (snow in July), unexpected (drought in Cambridge) and severe (heatwaves causing deaths).
  • It's getting more frequent: Met Office extreme events went from 3 in 2000 to 12 in 2020.
  • It's getting more severe: the high temperature record from 1911 lasted 79 years, 1990's lasted 13 years, 2019's only 3 years.

In depth: global atmospheric circulation

  1. The Sun heats the equator most. Warm air rises, creating low pressure and lots of rain (rainforests).
  2. The air cools, spreads north and south, and sinks at about 30°, creating high pressure: dry, clear skies (hot deserts).
  3. Surface winds blow from high to low pressure: the trade winds back to the equator and the westerlies towards 60°.
  4. At about 60° warm and cold air meet and rise (low pressure, wet: the UK). At the poles cold air sinks (high pressure, dry).
  5. These loops are the Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells. They explain where deserts, rainforests and tropical storms are found.

In depth: how a tropical storm forms

  1. The sea is at least 26–27 °C, in late summer and autumn, between about 5° and 30° from the equator.
  2. Warm, moist air rises quickly, creating an area of very low pressure at the surface.
  3. As the air rises it cools and condenses into towering cumulonimbus clouds. Condensation releases heat, which powers the storm.
  4. Air rushes in at the surface to replace the rising air. The Coriolis effect (Earth's spin) makes it spin, anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere. This is why storms don't form right on the equator.
  5. Cool, dry air sinks in the centre, forming the calm, clear eye. The eye wall around it has the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
  6. The storm moves with the prevailing winds, growing over warm sea. It weakens over land or cold water because it loses its supply of warm, moist air.

Hazards: winds over 119 km/h, storm surges (the sea pushed onto land by wind and low pressure; Haiyan's was over 5 m), heavy rain, flooding and landslides.

Climate change: warmer seas could mean storms are more intense and found further from the equator. The number of storms may not rise.

6-mark plan: "Explain how tropical storms form."
  1. Conditions: sea above 26 °C, 5–30° latitude.
  2. Rising warm, moist air leads to low pressure.
  3. Condensation releases heat and powers the storm.
  4. The Coriolis effect makes it spin; the eye and eye wall form.
  5. It weakens over land because it loses its energy source. Use cause-and-effect words throughout: "this causes…", "as a result…".

Climate change

From the AQA specification.

Evidence

  • Ice cores: air bubbles show past CO₂ and temperature, going back 800 000 years.
  • Tree rings: wider rings in warm, wet years.
  • Temperature records since about 1850 show warming of around 1 °C.
  • Shrinking glaciers and sea ice; rising sea level.

Causes

  • Natural: changes in Earth's orbit (Milankovitch cycles), volcanic eruptions (ash blocks the sun, so cooling), sunspots (more sunspots, more solar energy).
  • Greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases trap heat that would escape into space. The enhanced greenhouse effect is when humans add more of these gases.
  • Human: burning fossil fuels (CO₂), agriculture (methane from cattle and rice paddies), deforestation (fewer trees absorbing CO₂, plus CO₂ released when trees are burned).

Effects

  • People: coastal flooding from sea level rise, more extreme weather, crop failures, water shortages, more disease in warmer areas.
  • Environment: melting ice, coral bleaching, species losing habitats.

Managing it

  • Mitigation (reduce the causes): alternative energy (wind, solar), carbon capture and storage, planting trees, international agreements (Paris Agreement, 2015).
  • Adaptation (live with the effects): drought-resistant crops, managing water supply (reservoirs, hosepipe bans), coastal defences against sea level rise.

In depth: the greenhouse effect, step by step

  1. Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and heats the Earth's surface.
  2. The Earth gives off heat as long-wave radiation.
  3. Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, water vapour) absorb some of it and send it back to Earth, keeping the planet warm. This natural effect makes life possible.
  4. Humans add more greenhouse gases, so more heat is trapped: the enhanced greenhouse effect, which causes global warming.

  3. Judgement: "Human activity is the main cause because the warming since 1950 is too fast and too large to be explained by natural causes."

Ecosystems

  • Ecosystem: the links between plants, animals, climate and the land. Global scale (hot desert) or small scale (a pond, a woodland).
  • Biotic = living (trees, fish). Abiotic = non-living (soil, light, water).
  • Producers (plants) photosynthesise; consumers eat them or other consumers; decomposers (bacteria, fungi, worms) break down dead material.
  • Food chains and webs show energy moving from producers to consumers. Arrows point to the eater.
  • Pyramid of biomass: biomass falls at each level because energy is used for movement, respiration and so on, not just growth.
  • Nutrient cycle: soil → biomass (plants absorb) → litter (they die) → soil (decomposition). Stores are Soil, Biomass, Litter.
  • Interdependence: change one part (e.g. fewer beetles) and it affects the rest (fewer woodpeckers, then fewer owls).
  • Biome: a global-scale ecosystem, e.g. tropical rainforest, hot desert, tundra.

In depth: how an ecosystem works

  • Producers (plants) turn sunlight into energy. Consumers eat them (primary), and are eaten in turn (secondary, tertiary). Decomposers (fungi, bacteria) break down dead matter.
  • Food webs show how many food chains link together, so a change to one species affects others.
  • Nutrient cycling: nutrients pass from the soil to the biomass (living things) and the litter (dead leaves), then back to the soil as things decompose.

Example of change: if ponds in a woodland are drained, plants that need water die, so the insects and frogs that eat them decline, then the birds that eat those decline. Each change ripples through the food web.

6-mark plan: "Explain how a change to one part of an ecosystem affects other parts." Name the change, follow it through the food web step by step (producer, then consumer, then predator), and include the effect on nutrient cycling. Use a real example such as British woodland.

Tropical rainforests

Characteristics

  • Between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (Amazon, Malaysia). The sun hits directly, so it's hot (about 27 °C all year). A low pressure belt makes it wet (over 2 000 mm rain a year). So biodiversity is very high.
  • Layers: emergent (tallest trees, e.g. tualang), canopy (a roof that blocks light; most animals live here), under canopy (saplings wait for gaps), shrub layer (dark; ferns with large leaves).
  • Nutrient cycle: most nutrients are in the biomass. Few in the soil (fast uptake, heavy rain leaches them) and few in the litter (it decomposes quickly).

Adaptations

  • Drip tips (weeping fig): rain runs off so leaves don't break.
  • Buttress roots (tualang): support tall trees.
  • Lianas and climbers (vanilla orchid): reach the light in the canopy while rooted in soil.
  • Epiphytes (bromeliads): grow on branches and collect water in overlapping leaves.
  • Large leaves (giant taro): absorb more light in the dark shrub layer.

Deforestation

  • Causes: logging (clear-felling hardwoods like teak), subsistence farming (slash and burn), commercial farming (plantations), mineral extraction (open-cast mines), energy (HEP dams flood land), settlements and population growth, road building (opens up more forest).
  • Impacts: soil erosion (no roots to bind the soil; nutrients leached), local climate change (less transpiration, so drier), global climate change (less CO₂ absorbed), but also economic development (jobs, exports, tax).

Value and sustainable management

  • Value: medicines, food, biodiversity, regulating climate (local and global), indigenous homes.
  • Selective logging: only old trees cut so the forest recovers (but slower and more expensive).
  • Replanting: seeds from primary forest grow secondary forest.
  • Conservation areas such as national parks (hard to police). Education so people buy fewer products linked to deforestation.
  • Ecotourism: small, locally owned resorts; locals keep the profit so they don't need to log or mine.
  • International hardwood agreements: only certified logs traded (International Tropical Timber Agreement, 1983; 62 countries by 2006).
  • Debt reduction: HICs cancel debt if LICs spend the money on conservation.

In depth: how the rainforest works

  • Climate: hot (about 27 °C) and wet (over 2000 mm of rain a year) all year round, because it's at the equator under low pressure.
  • Layers: emergent, canopy, under-canopy and shrub layer, competing for light.
  • Plant adaptations: drip-tip leaves shed heavy rain; buttress roots support tall trees in shallow soil; lianas climb towards the light.
  • Animal adaptations: e.g. sloths move slowly to save energy and are camouflaged with algae.
  • Fast nutrient cycle: leaves decompose quickly in the heat and damp, and plants absorb the nutrients straight away. The soil itself is poor once trees are removed, as heavy rain washes the nutrients out (leaching).

In depth: causes and effects of deforestation

  1. Causes: commercial farming (cattle, palm oil), logging, mineral extraction (Carajás iron mine), roads, energy (HEP dams), settlement and population growth (see Malaysia).
  2. Economic effects: + jobs, exports and money for development; − loss of future income from tourism and medicines.
  3. Environmental effects: soil erosion, loss of biodiversity, and less CO₂ absorbed, so more climate change. Burning releases even more CO₂.

Sustainable management: selective logging, replanting, conservation and ecotourism, education, international agreements (e.g. on hardwood), and debt reduction (debt-for-nature swaps).

9-mark plan: "To what extent do the benefits of deforestation outweigh the costs?" Use your case study: give the economic benefits with data, then the environmental and social costs, then judge whether the benefits are short-term while the costs are long-term. End with a clear conclusion.
BDs

Hot deserts

Characteristics

  • Between 15° and 30° N and S (Thar, Sahara, Atacama). Sun hits directly, so hot; a high pressure belt means air sinks and clouds rarely form, so dry (under 250 mm rain a year). Biodiversity is low.
  • High diurnal temperature range: hot days, cold nights, because there are no clouds to keep the heat in.
  • Nutrient cycle: most nutrients are in the soil. Few plants absorb them and little rain leaches them.

Adaptations

  • Camel: fatty hump (energy), wide feet (don't sink), two rows of eyelashes and thin nostrils (sand), thick fur on top only.
  • Fennec fox: nocturnal (avoids heat, loses less water), large ears (lose heat).
  • Kangaroo rat: light fur reflects heat; very concentrated urine saves water.
  • Plants: cacti store water in thick stems, have spines instead of leaves and long or wide roots. Many have waxy skins to cut water loss.

Desertification

  • Desertification: soil on the fringe of a hot desert becomes dry and degraded (loses nutrients).
  • Causes: population growth leading to over-grazing, over-cultivation and deforestation for firewood; climate change (hotter, less rain). With no plants to bind and shade it, soil dries out and its nutrients wash away.
  • Reducing the risk: planting trees (roots bind soil, leaves shade it), ponding banks and contour traps (stone lines that trap water and soil), zaï pits filled with compost.

In depth: hot deserts

  • Climate: under 250 mm of rain a year, because air sinks at about 30° (high pressure, no clouds). Very hot days but cold nights, because there are no clouds to trap heat.
  • Plant adaptations: cacti store water in their stems, have spines instead of leaves to reduce water loss, and have long or wide roots.
  • Animal adaptations: camels store fat in their humps and lose little water; many animals are nocturnal.

In depth: desertification

  1. Causes: climate change (less rain), population growth, overgrazing, over-cultivation, removing wood for fuel, and over-abstraction of water.
  2. Vegetation is lost, so roots no longer hold the soil together.
  3. Bare soil is eroded by wind and rain and loses its nutrients, so land turns to desert.

Reducing the risk: water and soil management, tree planting (the Great Green Wall in the Sahel), stone lines (magic stones) to catch run-off, and appropriate technology that local people can afford and repair.

9-mark plan: "Do the opportunities of a hot desert outweigh the challenges?" Use the Thar Desert. Opportunities: mineral extraction, energy (solar and wind), farming with irrigation (the Indira Gandhi Canal), tourism. Challenges: extreme heat, water supply, poor access. Then judge: opportunities can outweigh the challenges where investment like the canal overcomes them.

Fetch = the distance the wind blows over. Longer fetch means more powerful waves.

Processes

  • Erosion: hydraulic power (air forced into cracks), abrasion (rocks thrown at the cliff), attrition (rocks knock together and get smaller and rounder).
  • Weathering: mechanical (freeze-thaw: water freezes in cracks and expands), chemical (rainwater dissolves limestone), biological (roots and burrowing animals).
  • Mass movement: sliding, slumping (rotational, on soft wet clay), rock fall.
  • Longshore drift: waves approach at an angle of the prevailing wind; swash carries sediment up at an angle; backwash pulls it straight down; sediment zig-zags along the coast.

Landforms

  • Headlands and bays: alternating hard and soft rock. Soft rock erodes faster into bays; hard rock is left sticking out as headlands.
  • Cave → arch → stack → stump: a crack in a headland is widened by hydraulic power and abrasion into a cave; it erodes through into an arch; the roof is weathered and collapses, leaving a stack; the stack is eroded into a stump.
  • Wave-cut platform: erosion between high and low tide makes a wave-cut notch; the cliff above collapses; repeated, the cliff retreats and leaves a flat rocky platform.
  • Beaches (sand in sheltered bays, shingle on exposed coasts) and sand dunes (wind blows dry sand inland; marram grass traps it).
  • Spit: longshore drift carries sediment past a bend in the coast or a river mouth; it builds out to sea; a change in wind direction curves the end; salt marsh forms behind it.
  • Bar: a spit grows right across a bay, trapping a lagoon behind it.

In depth: landform formation, step by step

Cave → arch → stack → stump (e.g. Old Harry Rocks, Dorset):

  1. A headland has a weakness such as a crack or joint.
  2. Hydraulic power and abrasion widen the crack into a cave.
  3. The cave erodes right through the headland to form an arch (Durdle Door).
  4. The base of the arch is eroded and weathering weakens the roof until it collapses, leaving a stack.
  5. The stack is eroded at its base until it collapses into a stump, covered at high tide.

Spit (e.g. Spurn Head, Hurst Castle):

  1. Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind.
  2. Where the coast changes direction (a bend or river mouth), the sediment is deposited out to sea.
  3. Over time it builds into a long ridge of sand or shingle attached to the land at one end.
  4. Changes in wind direction curve the end into a recurved hook.
  5. Sheltered water behind the spit becomes a salt marsh.

Rivers

Profiles

Upper courseMiddle courseLower course
ValleySteep, V-shapedWide, gently slopingVery wide, almost flat
ChannelNarrow, shallowWider, deeperVery wide and deep
Main processVertical erosionLateral erosion and depositionDeposition

The long profile shows the river getting less steep from source to mouth.

Processes

  • Erosion: hydraulic action (force of water), abrasion (rocks scrape the bed and banks), attrition (rocks knock together, smaller and rounder), solution (water dissolves rock). Vertical erosion deepens; lateral erosion widens.
  • Transportation: traction (large boulders rolled), saltation (pebbles bounced), suspension (fine particles carried), solution (dissolved minerals).
  • Deposition: when the river loses energy (slower, shallower, at the mouth).

Landforms

  • Interlocking spurs: in the upper course the river winds around hard rock it can't erode, leaving spurs.
  • Waterfall and gorge: hard rock lies over soft rock; the soft rock erodes faster, making an overhang and a plunge pool; the overhang collapses; the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
  • Meander: the fastest flow on the outside bend erodes a river cliff; slow flow on the inside deposits a slip-off slope.
  • Ox-bow lake: erosion narrows the meander's neck; in a flood the river cuts through; deposition seals off the old loop.
  • Flood plain: flat land either side of the river, built up from silt deposited in floods.
  • Levées: in a flood the heaviest sediment is dropped first, next to the channel; repeated floods build raised banks.
  • Estuary: the tidal mouth of a river where mudflats form.

Flooding

  • A river floods when discharge (water flowing) is greater than capacity (what the channel holds).
  • Physical factors: heavy or prolonged precipitation, impermeable geology (clay, granite), steep relief. Human factor: land use (urban concrete is impermeable; deforestation means less interception).
  • Hydrograph: bars = rainfall, line = discharge. Lag time is the time from peak rainfall to peak discharge. Short lag time = high flood risk.

In depth: river landforms, step by step

Waterfall and gorge (e.g. High Force on the River Tees):

  1. The river flows over a band of hard rock lying on top of soft rock.
  2. The soft rock erodes faster (hydraulic action, abrasion), creating a step.
  3. Water falls over the step and erodes a deep plunge pool at the bottom.
  4. The soft rock under the hard rock is undercut, leaving an overhang.
  5. The overhang becomes unsupported and collapses; the rocks fall into the plunge pool and cause more abrasion.
  6. This repeats, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.

Meander to ox-bow lake:

  1. The fastest water is on the outside of a bend, causing lateral erosion and a river cliff.
  2. Slower water on the inside deposits sediment, forming a slip-off slope.
  3. The bends get bigger and the neck between them narrows.
  4. During a flood, the river cuts through the neck and takes the shortest route.
  5. Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving an ox-bow lake.

Floodplain and levées: when the river floods it slows down and drops sediment. The heaviest sediment is dropped first, right by the channel. Over many floods this builds raised banks called levées, and the fine silt spread further away builds a flat, fertile floodplain.

In depth: what makes flooding more likely

  • Physical: heavy or long rainfall, impermeable rock (e.g. clay), steep slopes, already-saturated ground.
  • Human: urbanisation (concrete is impermeable, and drains carry water quickly), deforestation (less interception), farming.
  • Hydrographs: a short lag time and high peak discharge mean a "flashy" river and more flood risk (e.g. Boscastle 2004: steep, narrow valley and extreme rain).
6-mark plan: "Explain how a waterfall forms." Follow the six steps in order with the key terms (hard/soft rock, undercutting, overhang, plunge pool, retreat, gorge). Name an example like High Force. A labelled sketch can earn marks.

Urban trends

Key terms

  • Urban: a built-up area (town or city). Rural: countryside.
  • Urbanisation: an increase in the percentage of people living in urban areas.
  • Urban growth: an increase in the population of a city. Urban sprawl: an increase in the physical size of a city.
  • Megacity: a city with 10 million or more people.

Global patterns

  • In 1950 about 30% of the world's people lived in urban areas. The figure passed 50% in 2007, is about 57% now, and is predicted to reach about 68% by 2050.
  • HICs (e.g. UK, USA) are already about 80% urban, so they're urbanising slowly. Most people already live in cities.
  • NEEs (e.g. Brazil, China, India) are urbanising rapidly because industry is creating jobs in cities.
  • LICs (e.g. much of sub-Saharan Africa) have the lowest % urban but the fastest urban growth.
  • Most megacities are in Asia (e.g. Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai) and in NEEs and LICs. There are few in Europe and none in Australia.

Why cities grow

Rural-urban migrationNatural increase
Push factors (rural): few jobs, low farm income, drought, poor schools and healthcare, conflict.
Pull factors (urban): more jobs and better pay, schools, hospitals, family already there, a "better life".		More births than deaths. Migrants are often young adults, so they have children in the city. Better healthcare in cities lowers the death rate.

The UK's population

  • Uneven distribution. Most densely populated: London and the south-east, the Midlands (Birmingham) and the north-west (Manchester, Liverpool), plus Leeds, Newcastle in the north-east, and Scotland's central belt between Edinburgh and Glasgow.
  • Sparsely populated: the Scottish Highlands, mid-Wales and upland areas, where relief is steep and building is difficult and expensive.
  • Reasons: many big cities are on the coast (ports bring jobs in trade and fishing). Northern cities grew near coal in the Industrial Revolution.
  • Today the fastest growth is in the south, especially cities near London such as Oxford, Reading and Cambridge. London attracts international investment and has direct links to Europe, so it starts a multiplier effect that benefits nearby cities.

In depth: describing graphs and maps of urbanisation

Use TEA: the Trend ("urban population increased"), Examples with numbers and place names ("from 0.7 billion in 1950 to 4 billion in 2015"), and Anomalies (the place or year that doesn't fit).

For a map of megacities, use compass directions and continents: "Most megacities are in Asia, for example Tokyo and Delhi. There are several in South America, such as São Paulo, but none in Australia."

4-mark plan: "Explain why urbanisation is happening rapidly in LICs and NEEs." Give one push factor and one pull factor, and extend each ("…so people move to the city for factory jobs that pay more"). Then add natural increase: young migrants have children, and better healthcare means fewer deaths.
URi

Rio de Janeiro (city in an NEE)

Location and importance

  • On the south-east coast of Brazil, South America, beside the Atlantic Ocean. It lies south-east of Brasília, the capital. Brazil is an NEE.
  • Regional: banks in Rio provide services to businesses in nearby towns and rural areas. Many migrants come from the surrounding rural areas.
  • National: Brazil's second-largest city; produces about 5% of Brazil's GNI. It is the "cultural capital", with 50+ museums and the carnival. Coffee, sugar and iron ore are exported from its ports.
  • International: Christ the Redeemer (a new wonder of the world), hosted the 2014 World Cup final and the 2016 Olympics. It has 5 ports and 3 airports, international bank offices and lots of tourism.

Causes of growth

  • Rural-urban migration: about 65% of people in Rio are migrants, pulled by jobs and services.
  • Natural increase: young migrants have children, and healthcare in the city lowers the death rate.

Opportunities

  • Social: more people means more workers and more tax, which pays for better services. Rio has 105 hospitals, 300 km of new water pipes (95% of homes have running water), and 1 000 primary and 400 secondary schools, so life expectancy and literacy have improved.
  • Economic: the multiplier effect. Factories like the VW factory create jobs in linked industries; every 2 jobs in steel create about 13 more. Extra tax paid for the BRT (Bus Rapid Transit) system.

Challenges

  • Favelas: low-income migrants build homes illegally on steep land at the edge of the city (e.g. Rocinha, the largest). They're at risk from landslides.
  • Services: 12% of favela homes have no water, 50% have no sewer connection and 30% have no electricity.
  • Health: narrow roads stop waste collection, so disease spreads. Infant mortality is about 50 per 1 000.
  • Crime: drug gangs control many favelas, with a murder rate of about 20 per 1 000.
  • Unemployment: about 20% in favelas. Many work in the informal economy for less than £60 a month.
  • Pollution and congestion: more cars mean air pollution that kills about 5 000 people a year.

Urban planning: the Favela Bairro project

  • Aimed to improve quality of life in favelas such as Complexo do Alemão, instead of knocking them down.
  • Roads widened and paved, so waste lorries and emergency services can get in.
  • Daycare centres and adult education, so literacy improves and parents have time to earn money.
  • Cable car linking hilltop favelas to the city's train line, with a free daily return trip for residents to reach jobs.
  • Police Pacifying Units (UPPs) cleared drug gangs from some favelas.
  • Was it successful? Quality of life improved in the favelas involved, but it cost about $1 billion for just a few of them. The cable car has since closed and other infrastructure wasn't maintained. Gangs returned to some areas.

In depth: chains of explanation for Rio

  1. Migration → favelas: migrants can't afford formal housing, so they build on unused steep land. The homes are self-built, so they have no planning, sewers or electricity.
  2. No sewers → disease: waste goes into open drains and narrow roads stop bin lorries, so water gets contaminated. Diseases like diarrhoea spread, which explains the high infant mortality rate.
  3. Informal jobs → poverty: informal work pays no tax and has no contracts, so incomes are low and the city gets less money for services.
  4. Growth → multiplier effect: a new factory creates jobs. Workers spend money, which creates more jobs in shops and services, and more tax pays for things like the BRT.
6-mark plan: "Using a named example, explain how urban growth has created social and economic opportunities." Rio. Paragraph 1, social: more tax → 105 hospitals, 95% running water → longer life expectancy. Paragraph 2, social: 1 000+ schools → higher literacy → better jobs. Paragraph 3, economic: the multiplier effect (VW, steel 2 → 13 jobs) → tax for the BRT. Extend every point with "this means that…".
9-mark plan: "To what extent has urban planning improved quality of life for the urban poor?" Judgement first ("To a large extent, but only for a few favelas"). Supporting points: wider roads mean waste collection and ambulances, which lowers deaths; the cable car gives access to jobs; daycare and adult education raise literacy. Counter: it cost about $1bn for a few favelas, the cable car closed, things weren't maintained and gangs returned. Conclusion: repeat the judgement. +3 SPaG.
UCa

Cambridge (UK city)

Location and importance

  • In the East of England, in the county of Cambridgeshire, about 80 km north of London. It's inland, on the River Cam.
  • Regional: Crown Court; shopping and leisure (Grand Arcade, the Junction) used by people from nearby towns.
  • National: Royal Papworth Hospital is the UK's leading heart and lung transplant centre. The Fitzwilliam Museum is one of the UK's top museums.
  • International: the University is consistently ranked in the world top 5. About 7.6 million tourists visit each year, worth about £2.2 billion.

Migration and the city's character

  • The University has about 24 000 students, and 41% are from outside the UK. This means more student accommodation (e.g. at the end of Victoria and Histon Road), university events (rowing, conferences), and research businesses on the Science Park and Biomedical Campus.
  • Science businesses create jobs and pay more tax for local government, which improves infrastructure: the multiplier effect.
  • International migrants: in 2018 most new National Insurance registrations in Cambridge came from Europe (63%). By 2024 most came from Asia (55%), with only 12% from Europe.
  • Mill Road is now very culturally diverse, with shops, restaurants and services for ethnic minorities and the Cambridge Central Mosque.

Opportunities of urban change

  • Social: regeneration (the old Cattle Market became a leisure park with a cinema and bowling). New schools, such as the University of Cambridge Primary School and the Cambridge Maths School, lead to better qualifications.
  • Economic: new transport infrastructure (Cambridge North station, 2017) gives job access for about 50 000 commuters. Growth of 21 000+ people since 2011 provides workers for businesses like AstraZeneca.
  • Environmental: urban greening. 2 000 trees were planted in Eddington phase one, giving cleaner air and parks for exercise.

Challenges of urban change

  • Inequality: Newnham (west, by the river) and Abbey (east) are very different. Almost twice as many Newnham residents have 5+ GCSEs, and almost twice as many work in professional jobs (doctors, lawyers). Houses in Abbey are smaller and closer together.
  • Waste: about 400 000 tonnes a year, with half sent to landfill. That risks leachate polluting water, and the Waterbeach landfill is almost full. A proposed £100m incinerator (300 jobs, burning 250 000 tonnes a year, electricity for 63 000 homes) faced strong local objections.
  • Urban sprawl onto the rural-urban fringe: Orchard Park was built on a greenfield site, causing habitat loss, lower biodiversity and higher flood risk (impermeable surfaces → more surface runoff). Trumpington Meadows brought more traffic on the edge of the city, so deliveries are late and businesses earn less profit.
  • Brownfield = previously built-on land (cheaper to protect countryside, but may need cleaning up). Greenfield = never built on (easier and cheaper to build, but loses countryside).
  • Congestion: growth plus narrow medieval streets. Emergency services are slower, workers and deliveries are late, and there's more air pollution.

Regeneration: the CB1 project (Station Road)

  • Why: the area around the station looked unattractive (empty buildings, derelict land) and gave tourists a bad first impression. There wasn't enough cycle parking, and taxis, buses, cyclists and pedestrians shared one space, which risked accidents.
  • What: developer Brookgate. New housing (Mill Park Road), a cycle park with about 3 000 spaces, wider pavements, more bus stops, new businesses (Clayton Hotel, Microsoft Research offices) and urban greening.
  • Successful? The built environment improved and looks better for tourists and businesses; locals say it is much cleaner. But there is anti-social behaviour (noisy students), and short-term rental flats have been misused, so police patrols had to increase.

In depth: explaining inequality

  1. Fewer qualifications in Abbey → fewer professional jobs → lower income.
  2. Lower income → smaller, more crowded housing and less money for healthy food and activities → worse health and lower life expectancy.
  3. Cambridge house prices are very high (driven by the university and tech jobs), so lower-paid workers are pushed into cheaper areas or commute in.
9-mark plan: "To what extent has urban change created opportunities in a UK city you have studied?" Judgement (e.g. "to a large extent, but not for everyone"). Point 1: economic, Cambridge North station and jobs at AstraZeneca and the Science Park. Point 2: social or environmental, the leisure park, new schools, urban greening in Eddington. Point 3, counter: inequality between Newnham and Abbey (2× GCSEs and professionals), so opportunities aren't shared equally. Conclusion: "Overall…". PEEL every paragraph.
6-mark plan: "Evaluate the success of an urban regeneration project you have studied." CB1: why it was needed, then successes (3 000 cycle spaces, safer separated transport, new businesses and jobs, cleaner) versus problems (anti-social behaviour, misuse of short-term lets, mostly expensive new flats). Finish with a judgement.

Sustainable cities and transport

Sustainable urban living

Meeting the needs of people now without stopping future generations meeting theirs. That means saving water and energy, recycling waste, creating green space and using public transport.

Eddington (north-west Cambridge)

  • Energy: triple-glazed, well-insulated homes, some with solar panels and green roofs. A district heating system heats every home from one energy centre, which is more efficient and cheaper, and homes don't need boilers.
  • Water: a sustainable drainage system. Rain runs through grassy swales into a lake and is reused as non-drinking water, so about 40% less water is pumped in and flood risk is lower.
  • Waste: one of the UK's largest underground bin systems (about 450 chutes). Sensors tell the council when the bins are full, so lorries only collect when needed.
  • Transport: a frequent bus to the centre and station, plus wide cycleways, so fewer car journeys and fossil fuels are burned.
  • Green space and community: 2 000+ trees in phase one, play areas, an outdoor gym, nesting features for birds, a Sainsbury's (jobs), a primary school and the Storey's Field Centre.

Reducing traffic congestion in Cambridge

StrategyExamples
Make parking difficultGrand Arcade car park is about £3.60 an hour; resident-only parking (e.g. Courtney Way)
Make driving slower20 mph limits (Arbury Road); bus lanes (Elizabeth Way, Hills Road)
Offer alternativesPark & Ride, Cambridge North station, cycle lanes (Milton Road), the Guided Busway

Successful? Cycling replaces about 38 000 car journeys a day, but the Guided Busway only cut A14 traffic by about 2% against a predicted 11%.

Northstowe (commuter settlement)

  • A new town north-west of Cambridge, built because of the high demand for housing. Most residents work in Cambridge and commute by car (A14) or the Guided Busway.
  • Pros: less urban sprawl of Cambridge itself, more passengers for the busway. Cons: more A14 traffic, few leisure facilities and services yet, so a weaker sense of community and loneliness.
6-mark plan: "Assess how far urban areas can be made more sustainable." Eddington: energy (district heating, triple glazing), water (swales, lake, 40% less water), waste (sensor bins), transport (buses, cycleways). Counter: it's one new development; most of Cambridge is older housing that's harder to change, and the homes are expensive. Judgement.

PEEL paragraphs
  1. Point: answer the question using its words.
  2. Example: "For example…" with a place and a number from your case study.
  3. Extend: "This means that… As a result… so…". This is where the marks are.
  4. Link: "Therefore…" back to the question.
Example
Wealth affects how many people die in an earthquake. For example, in Nepal 9 000 people died. This means that poorly built homes collapsed and landslides blocked roads, so aid couldn't reach people. Therefore poorer countries suffer more deaths.

A good answer is clear (could anyone follow it?), specific (key terms, real numbers) and extended (goes beyond the basic point).
