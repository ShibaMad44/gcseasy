ATc

Tectonic hazards

Natural hazards and risk

  • Natural hazard: an extreme natural event that could harm people or damage property. It's only a hazard if people live there. A natural disaster is one that has already happened.
  • Three types: atmospheric (weather), geological (land moving), flooding (water).
  • Hazard risk = the chance of being affected. It depends on location, wealth (HICs afford protection and monitoring but have more valuable property), education (e.g. earthquake drills in Japanese schools), urbanisation (more people and tall buildings close together) and climate change (more sea above 26 °C).

Plate tectonics

  • Earth: core (hottest), mantle (magma), crust (solid rock), split into tectonic plates. Continental plates are thicker and less dense; oceanic plates are thinner and denser.
  • Plates move because of convection currents in the mantle: magma near the core heats, rises, spreads under the crust, cools and sinks. Higher grades: also ridge push and slab pull (gravity).
  • Earthquakes and volcanoes are in belts along plate margins, e.g. the Pacific Ring of Fire and the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Some aren't, like Hawaii.

Effects and responses

  • Primary effects are caused by the hazard itself (ground shaking: deaths, collapsed buildings). Secondary effects are caused by the primary effects (tsunami, landslides, fires, homelessness, disease).
  • Immediate responses: hours to weeks, temporary (rescue, aid, shelters). Long-term responses: months to years, permanent (rebuilding, new laws, warning systems).

Why people live with the risk

  • Geothermal energy is cheap (65% of Iceland's energy).
  • Fertile volcanic soil for farming (olives around Mt Vesuvius).
  • Minerals to mine (copper at Mt St Helens).
  • Tourism (Hawaii's tourism worth over $15 billion).
  • Family and friends, lack of money to move, or the risk feels managed.

AWt

Weather hazards

Global atmospheric circulation

  • Warm air rises → low pressure (e.g. the Equator). Cool air sinks → high pressure (e.g. the poles, 30° N and S).
  • Wind blows from high to low pressure (trade winds blow from 30° to the Equator). Winds curve because of Earth's rotation: the Coriolis effect.
  • Rain: warm water evaporates, air rises, cools, water vapour condenses into clouds, precipitation falls. Wet places are in low pressure belts (the UK, about 55° N); dry places in high pressure belts (hot deserts, 15–30° N and S).

Tropical storms

  • Large, spinning, very low pressure storms. Called hurricanes (Atlantic, NE Pacific), typhoons (NW Pacific), cyclones (Indian Ocean, S Pacific).
  • Form 5–15° north and south of the Equator. Never at the Equator: the Coriolis effect is too weak there.
  • Need: sea at 26 °C or warmer, very low pressure, enough Coriolis effect to spin, low wind shear to stay upright.
  1. Warm sea water (26 °C+) evaporates and rises quickly.
  2. It cools and condenses into tall cumulonimbus clouds.
  3. Storms join into one 480–650 km wide and up to 8 km tall.
  4. The Coriolis effect spins it. Winds of 75 mph or more; over 100 mph at the eye wall.
  5. Cool air sinks in the centre, making the calm, cloudless eye.
  6. The prevailing wind moves it. Over land it loses energy (friction, no warm water).

Climate change could change distribution (further from the Equator), frequency (more often) and intensity (faster winds, more rain), because more sea is above 26 °C for longer. The evidence is mixed.

Reducing risk: satellite thermal maps and tracking (monitoring and prediction; cones of uncertainty), evacuation routes, warnings and survival kits (planning), storm shelters on stilts with shutters, made of concrete (protection).

Extreme weather in the UK

  • Extreme weather is unseasonal (snow in July), unexpected (drought in Cambridge) and severe (heatwaves causing deaths).
  • It's getting more frequent: Met Office extreme events went from 3 in 2000 to 12 in 2020.
  • It's getting more severe: the high temperature record from 1911 lasted 79 years, 1990's lasted 13 years, 2019's only 3 years.
ACl

Climate change

From the AQA specification.

Evidence

  • Ice cores: air bubbles show past CO₂ and temperature, going back 800 000 years.
  • Tree rings: wider rings in warm, wet years.
  • Temperature records since about 1850 show warming of around 1 °C.
  • Shrinking glaciers and sea ice; rising sea level.

Causes

  • Natural: changes in Earth's orbit (Milankovitch cycles), volcanic eruptions (ash blocks the sun, so cooling), sunspots (more sunspots, more solar energy).
  • Greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases trap heat that would escape into space. The enhanced greenhouse effect is when humans add more of these gases.
  • Human: burning fossil fuels (CO₂), agriculture (methane from cattle and rice paddies), deforestation (fewer trees absorbing CO₂, plus CO₂ released when trees are burned).

Effects

  • People: coastal flooding from sea level rise, more extreme weather, crop failures, water shortages, more disease in warmer areas.
  • Environment: melting ice, coral bleaching, species losing habitats.

Managing it

  • Mitigation (reduce the causes): alternative energy (wind, solar), carbon capture and storage, planting trees, international agreements (Paris Agreement, 2015).
  • Adaptation (live with the effects): drought-resistant crops, managing water supply (reservoirs, hosepipe bans), coastal defences against sea level rise.
BEc

Ecosystems

  • Ecosystem: the links between plants, animals, climate and the land. Global scale (hot desert) or small scale (a pond, a woodland).
  • Biotic = living (trees, fish). Abiotic = non-living (soil, light, water).
  • Producers (plants) photosynthesise; consumers eat them or other consumers; decomposers (bacteria, fungi, worms) break down dead material.
  • Food chains and webs show energy moving from producers to consumers. Arrows point to the eater.
  • Pyramid of biomass: biomass falls at each level because energy is used for movement, respiration and so on, not just growth.
  • Nutrient cycle: soil → biomass (plants absorb) → litter (they die) → soil (decomposition). Stores are Soil, Biomass, Litter.
  • Interdependence: change one part (e.g. fewer beetles) and it affects the rest (fewer woodpeckers, then fewer owls).
  • Biome: a global-scale ecosystem, e.g. tropical rainforest, hot desert, tundra.
BRf

Tropical rainforests

Characteristics

  • Between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (Amazon, Malaysia). The sun hits directly, so it's hot (about 27 °C all year). A low pressure belt makes it wet (over 2 000 mm rain a year). So biodiversity is very high.
  • Layers: emergent (tallest trees, e.g. tualang), canopy (a roof that blocks light; most animals live here), under canopy (saplings wait for gaps), shrub layer (dark; ferns with large leaves).
  • Nutrient cycle: most nutrients are in the biomass. Few in the soil (fast uptake, heavy rain leaches them) and few in the litter (it decomposes quickly).

Adaptations

  • Drip tips (weeping fig): rain runs off so leaves don't break.
  • Buttress roots (tualang): support tall trees.
  • Lianas and climbers (vanilla orchid): reach the light in the canopy while rooted in soil.
  • Epiphytes (bromeliads): grow on branches and collect water in overlapping leaves.
  • Large leaves (giant taro): absorb more light in the dark shrub layer.

Deforestation

  • Causes: logging (clear-felling hardwoods like teak), subsistence farming (slash and burn), commercial farming (plantations), mineral extraction (open-cast mines), energy (HEP dams flood land), settlements and population growth, road building (opens up more forest).
  • Impacts: soil erosion (no roots to bind the soil; nutrients leached), local climate change (less transpiration, so drier), global climate change (less CO₂ absorbed), but also economic development (jobs, exports, tax).

Value and sustainable management

  • Value: medicines, food, biodiversity, regulating climate (local and global), indigenous homes.
  • Selective logging: only old trees cut so the forest recovers (but slower and more expensive).
  • Replanting: seeds from primary forest grow secondary forest.
  • Conservation areas such as national parks (hard to police). Education so people buy fewer products linked to deforestation.
  • Ecotourism: small, locally owned resorts; locals keep the profit so they don't need to log or mine.
  • International hardwood agreements: only certified logs traded (International Tropical Timber Agreement, 1983; 62 countries by 2006).
  • Debt reduction: HICs cancel debt if LICs spend the money on conservation.
BDs

Hot deserts

Characteristics

  • Between 15° and 30° N and S (Thar, Sahara, Atacama). Sun hits directly, so hot; a high pressure belt means air sinks and clouds rarely form, so dry (under 250 mm rain a year). Biodiversity is low.
  • High diurnal temperature range: hot days, cold nights, because there are no clouds to keep the heat in.
  • Nutrient cycle: most nutrients are in the soil. Few plants absorb them and little rain leaches them.

Adaptations

  • Camel: fatty hump (energy), wide feet (don't sink), two rows of eyelashes and thin nostrils (sand), thick fur on top only.
  • Fennec fox: nocturnal (avoids heat, loses less water), large ears (lose heat).
  • Kangaroo rat: light fur reflects heat; very concentrated urine saves water.
  • Plants: cacti store water in thick stems, have spines instead of leaves and long or wide roots. Many have waxy skins to cut water loss.

Desertification

  • Desertification: soil on the fringe of a hot desert becomes dry and degraded (loses nutrients).
  • Causes: population growth leading to over-grazing, over-cultivation and deforestation for firewood; climate change (hotter, less rain). With no plants to bind and shade it, soil dries out and its nutrients wash away.
  • Reducing the risk: planting trees (roots bind soil, leaves shade it), ponding banks and contour traps (stone lines that trap water and soil), zaï pits filled with compost.

Coasts

Fetch = the distance the wind blows over. Longer fetch means more powerful waves.

Processes

  • Erosion: hydraulic power (air forced into cracks), abrasion (rocks thrown at the cliff), attrition (rocks knock together and get smaller and rounder).
  • Weathering: mechanical (freeze-thaw: water freezes in cracks and expands), chemical (rainwater dissolves limestone), biological (roots and burrowing animals).
  • Mass movement: sliding, slumping (rotational, on soft wet clay), rock fall.
  • Longshore drift: waves approach at an angle of the prevailing wind; swash carries sediment up at an angle; backwash pulls it straight down; sediment zig-zags along the coast.

Landforms

  • Headlands and bays: alternating hard and soft rock. Soft rock erodes faster into bays; hard rock is left sticking out as headlands.
  • Cave → arch → stack → stump: a crack in a headland is widened by hydraulic power and abrasion into a cave; it erodes through into an arch; the roof is weathered and collapses, leaving a stack; the stack is eroded into a stump.
  • Wave-cut platform: erosion between high and low tide makes a wave-cut notch; the cliff above collapses; repeated, the cliff retreats and leaves a flat rocky platform.
  • Beaches (sand in sheltered bays, shingle on exposed coasts) and sand dunes (wind blows dry sand inland; marram grass traps it).
  • Spit: longshore drift carries sediment past a bend in the coast or a river mouth; it builds out to sea; a change in wind direction curves the end; salt marsh forms behind it.
  • Bar: a spit grows right across a bay, trapping a lagoon behind it.

Rivers

Profiles

ValleySteep, V-shapedWide, gently slopingVery wide, almost flat
ChannelNarrow, shallowWider, deeperVery wide and deep
Main processVertical erosionLateral erosion and depositionDeposition

The long profile shows the river getting less steep from source to mouth.

Processes

  • Erosion: hydraulic action (force of water), abrasion (rocks scrape the bed and banks), attrition (rocks knock together, smaller and rounder), solution (water dissolves rock). Vertical erosion deepens; lateral erosion widens.
  • Transportation: traction (large boulders rolled), saltation (pebbles bounced), suspension (fine particles carried), solution (dissolved minerals).
  • Deposition: when the river loses energy (slower, shallower, at the mouth).

Landforms

  • Interlocking spurs: in the upper course the river winds around hard rock it can't erode, leaving spurs.
  • Waterfall and gorge: hard rock lies over soft rock; the soft rock erodes faster, making an overhang and a plunge pool; the overhang collapses; the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
  • Meander: the fastest flow on the outside bend erodes a river cliff; slow flow on the inside deposits a slip-off slope.
  • Ox-bow lake: erosion narrows the meander's neck; in a flood the river cuts through; deposition seals off the old loop.
  • Flood plain: flat land either side of the river, built up from silt deposited in floods.
  • Levées: in a flood the heaviest sediment is dropped first, next to the channel; repeated floods build raised banks.
  • Estuary: the tidal mouth of a river where mudflats form.

Flooding

  • A river floods when discharge (water flowing) is greater than capacity (what the channel holds).
  • Physical factors: heavy or prolonged precipitation, impermeable geology (clay, granite), steep relief. Human factor: land use (urban concrete is impermeable; deforestation means less interception).
  • Hydrograph: bars = rainfall, line = discharge. Lag time is the time from peak rainfall to peak discharge. Short lag time = high flood risk.

PEEL paragraphs
  1. Point: answer the question using its words.
  2. Example: "For example…" with a place and a number from your case study.
  3. Extend: "This means that… As a result… so…". This is where the marks are.
  4. Link: "Therefore…" back to the question.
Example
Wealth affects how many people die in an earthquake. For example, in Nepal 9 000 people died. This means that poorly built homes collapsed and landslides blocked roads, so aid couldn't reach people. Therefore poorer countries suffer more deaths.

A good answer is clear (could anyone follow it?), specific (key terms, real numbers) and extended (goes beyond the basic point).

SPaG: capital letters for place names, full stops, key terms spelled right (subduction, deforestation, desertification).

Describing graphs and maps: TEA
  1. Trend: what's the overall pattern? "Forest cover decreased."
  2. Examples: quote numbers, dates and place names. "From 72% in 1990 to 52% in 2020."
  3. Anomalies: what doesn't fit? "Except in 2020, when it peaked at 12."
Landform questions

Write every step in order, with the process that causes each one.

  1. Start with the rock: "hard rock lies over soft rock…"
  2. Name the process: hydraulic action, abrasion, deposition…
  3. Use sequence words: first… next… then… finally…
  4. End with the landform and a named example (Old Harry, High Force).

A labelled diagram can earn marks too, but only if the labels explain.

Writing about the economy

Use these words to make economic points specific: workers, jobs, income, business, profit, tax, trade, export, import.

Example
The Carajás railway lets iron ore be exported to Asia, so the government earns more tax.
